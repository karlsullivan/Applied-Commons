Step-by-step box-style solar cooker built from a cardboard box, aluminum foil, and clear plastic - a low-cost, locally buildable, low-power preparation aid for off-grid cooking (rice, vegetables, stews, water). Helps off-grid and low-resource cooks. Maturity: well-documented, long-standing community with fully published materials and build steps.

Assembly

Tools: Sharp utility knife / box cutter, Pencil and ruler or measuring tape, Flat glue brush (to lay a thin glue coat before foil), Wire cutters (to trim and bend the reflector prop), Flat-black spray paint or black tempera, non-toxic when dry, White glue or wheat paste (>=250 g / 8 oz), Oven mitts and UV sunglasses (from a cited source, not the main build doc)
Skills: Straight, even cutting of cardboard, Folding and creasing corrugated edges into clean flaps, Gluing (thin glue coat, then smooth foil/plastic on without wrinkles), Basic hand-eye alignment of the two boxes and the lid, Painting a flat surface evenly and letting it dry fully, No electrical, mechanical, or soldering skills required
Time: Active assembly (cutting, folding, gluing, painting, glazing) is roughly 1-2 hours; the upstream doc calls the whole build 'a few hours' for very little money, with the main time sink being paint and glazing glue dry time before first use.

  1. Select and size the boxes Use an inner box at least 38 cm x 38 cm (15 in square); bigger is better. The outer box must be larger all around so there is >=1.5 cm (0.6 in) of airspace between the boxes; the gap need not be equal. Boxes can be resized by cutting and gluing.
    Caution: Choose clean, dry boxes free of residue; keep the knife ready for the cuts that follow.
  2. Trace and cut the opening in the outer box Fold the outer box's top flaps closed, set the inner box on top, and trace a line around it onto the outer box's top. Remove the inner box and cut along this line to form the opening.
    Caution: Cut on a stable surface away from the body; the blade is used repeatedly.
  3. Size the inner box and form the flaps Set the oven depth about 2.5 cm (1 in) deeper than the largest pot and 2.5 cm (1 in) shorter than the outer box so a gap remains between the box bottoms. Slit the inner box's corners down to that height, score a firm fold line between the cuts, and fold each side down into an extended flap.
    Caution: Corner-to-corner slit cuts; keep fingers behind the blade.
  4. Foil the inside of both boxes and the outer box's top flaps Lay a thin glue coat, then glue aluminum foil to the inside of the inner box, the inside of the outer box, and the inside of the outer box's remaining top flaps. Glue the outer box's top flaps closed. Neatness matters more on the inner box, which stays visible after assembly.
    Caution: Smooth foil to avoid loose edges and let the glue tack before handling. (Thin diluted-glue brush-on coat is a builder-reported technique from a second build guide.)
  5. Seat the inner box in the outer box Fill the outer box with wads of crumpled newspaper so that when the inner box is set into the top opening, its flaps just touch the top of the outer box. Glue those flaps onto the outer box's top and trim the excess even with the outer perimeter.
    Caution: This is the insulation / thermal-break step; no specific hazard.
  6. Make the black absorber base / drip pan Cut a piece of cardboard the same size as the interior bottom of the oven and foil one side. Paint the foiled side black and let it dry fully. Set it in the oven resting on the inner box's bottom, black side up; pots sit on it when cooking.
    Caution: Use flat-black paint labeled non-toxic when dry; ventilate while spraying and do not use the cooker until the paint is completely dry.
  7. Build the removable lid Lay the large lid sheet on top of the base, trace its outline, and cut and fold the edges down into a ~7.5 cm (3 in) lip. Fold the corner flaps around and glue to the side lid flaps. Orient the corrugations left-to-right as you face the oven so the prop can seat in them. Do NOT glue the lid to the box - it must lift off to load pots. A fit trick: rest the pencil against the box side when marking.
    Caution: Only the corner flaps are glued; the lid itself must stay unglued to the body.
  8. Form and foil the reflector flap Draw a rectangle on the lid the same size as the oven opening, cut around three sides, and fold the remaining flap up to form the reflector. Foil the inside of this flap.
    Caution: Foil the sun-facing inside side and smooth out wrinkles.
  9. Make the reflector prop Bend a 30 cm (12 in) length of hanger wire so it seats in the lid corrugations and props the reflector flap open at the working angle.
    Caution: The exact bend geometry is shown only in a figure (Figure 6), not in text - see Gaps.
  10. Glaze the lid Turn the lid upside down and glue the glazing in place. A turkey-size oven roasting bag (~47.5 x 58.5 cm / 19 x 23.5 in) can be applied unopened for a double plastic layer that separates into an air gap as it cooks; also glue the bag closed on its open end to stop condensation, or cut a bag open into a flat sheet covering the opening. Glass or plexiglas also work (more expensive/fragile).
    Caution: Oven bags are rated to ~204 C but are not UV-stable and embrittle over time, so replace as needed. The plastic glazing alone will not hold under wind or heat - tension it tightly and let the glue set (builder-reported detail from the Glazing page).
  11. (Optional) Improve efficiency Add foiled-cardboard liners in the wall spaces; make a full-lid reflector; or build the drip pan from black-painted sheet metal (aluminum flashing) elevated slightly off the bottom on small cardboard strips.
    Caution: Sheet-metal edges are sharp; deburr before handling.
  12. Check and first use Confirm the lid lifts on and off freely, the glazing is taut and sealed around the edges, the reflector flap folds and the prop seats, and the black base is fully dry. Place on a flat, non-combustible surface in direct sun to test.
    Caution: Wear sunglasses when adjusting the reflector (reflected glare); use oven mitts for hot pots; supervise children; place on stone or concrete, not dry grass or paper.

Safety

No electrical, battery, gas-combustion, radio, or patient-exposure hazards exist: the design is fully passive (cardboard + foil + clear plastic, solar-only). The hazards are heat-fire (enclosed box reaches ~120-160 C, up to 200 C well-insulated, vs paper autoignition ~218-246 C and the 204 C oven-bag glazing rating), pressure from sealed glass jars (rupture/shrapnel/scald), water-food/pasteurization limits (pasteurizes to ~65 C/149 F per WAPI but does not sterilize or remove chemical contaminants, and food held in the 4-60 C danger zone regrows bacteria), food-contact chemical (paint/glue/plastic at temperature), mechanical (utility-knife and sharp wire-prop cuts), and structural (wind knock-over of a lightweight hot box). The build pack already covers most of these (non-combustible surface, no light concentration, internal-temperature and danger-zone guidance, glass-jar venting, non-toxic paint spec, spray ventilation). Residual controls a builder must add: monitor while hot, blunt/tape wire-prop ends, replace degraded glazing, and stabilize against wind.

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • There is no PEP/S580.1 value for this exact DIY recipe; it was never tested. The SCI PEP table lists only 14 commercial cookers (24-117 W), and PEP testing costs $1,000 per unit and requires shipping to a test center (USA, Kenya, or Nepal).
  • Water pasteurization target: heating to 65 C for ~6 minutes inactivates all common waterborne pathogens (E. coli, rotavirus, Giardia, Hepatitis A); 1-log kill temps are protozoan cysts 55 C, E. coli/rotavirus 60 C, Hep A 65 C. Basis is the peer-reviewed Metcalf & Ciochetti 1984 box-cooker study (AEM 47:223-228) plus SCI/SSWM.
  • Under S580.1/PEP, standard cooking power is constructed to be independent of ambient temperature: it is read from a linear regression of cooking power versus (water-minus-ambient) temperature difference at a fixed 50 C, normalized to 700 W/m2, with >=30 observations over 3 days and r2 >= 0.75. The USEPA study confirms S580.1 results are replicable across ambient temperature and insolation; ambient temperature only constrains the test window (20-35 C recommended).
  • Humidity is not a variable in ASAE S580.1 or PEP at all - the protocol measures only water temperature, ambient air temperature, solar irradiance, and wind speed (plus GPS in PEP) - so the standardized SCP has no humidity dependence by construction.
  • The solarcooker.org DIY recipe is a box-type cardboard cooker (double box with newspaper/foam insulation, foil lining shiny-out, clear plastic glazing, black lidded pot, foil reflector flap), buildable for under $5 in itemized materials, with the site claiming 100-150 C operating temperatures (unaudited) and one field test (Steven Lim, Sri Damansara) reporting 135 C within 45 minutes. No measured watts are published for it anywhere on the site.
  • Holding water at 71 C exceeds the 65 C pasteurization threshold with comfortable margin; per the PACE temperature-time chart Salmonella is killed at 61.2 C within 0.1 h, so 71 C clears in well under 6 minutes.
  • No peer-reviewed or laboratory service-life study of cardboard box cookers was found (Crossref and Semantic Scholar searched; only design/performance papers exist). All service-life figures are community field reports, so treat the 6-month-to-2-year range as field anecdotes rather than measured data. Cooking cycles per se are not quantified anywhere as a failure driver; the literature attributes wear to moisture, UV, wind and outdoor storage.
  • Cheapest weatherization measure with the best cost-benefit: double glazing at zero added cost by gluing an UNOPENED turkey-size oven roasting bag over the box opening - the two plastic layers separate into a dead-air pocket (a passive double-pane), and gluing the bag's open end closed stops water vapor from entering and condensing. Alternatively, a sealed two-layer plastic 'double-pane' window (one layer on inner lid, one on outer lid, air pocket between) is reported to 'drastically boost performance' and also improves weather tolerance.
