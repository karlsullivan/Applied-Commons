The safety review found that parts of this build need a qualified person.

Global open-source (all content CC-BY) initiative that provides free blueprints and know-how so individuals and communities build their own small-scale plastic-recycling machines (shredder, extruder, injection molder, sheetpress) plus education/training videos and a global community platform, explicitly to recycle plastic locally instead of relying on large, inefficient plants. Meets 'local sourcing', 'no municipal infrastructure', and 'informal-sector workers' constraints. Mature (long-running, one of the largest open hardware communities).

Assembly

Tools: Lathe (turning the 27 mm hex drive shaft and motor coupling), Drill press + drill/bolt set (M-series bolts, nuts, washers), Welding machine (MIG/TIG) for brackets, shredding box, framework and hopper, Welding clamps / fixturing and a bench vise, Belt sander / belt grinder (de-burring and smoothing stainless edges), Laser (or plasma/water) cutter to cut the DXF parts - or outsourced to a cutter, Sheet-metal shears / bolt cutters (hopper from sheet steel), Wrenches, spanners, hammer, square, calipers, Multimeter and basic electrical hand tools, Welding PPE kit (eye/face shield, welding gloves, ventilation)
Skills: Welding (sheet steel; TIG/MIG) - all intermediate per the project, Machining / lathe work (shaft, motor coupling), Mechanical assembly (aligning power-to-cutting), Electronics (wiring a reversible switch, motor drive / VFD, 3-phase supply), Laser/DXF file preparation (or managing an outsourced cut), Basic sheet-metal fabrication (hopper)
Time: Community's own difficulty tag: '< 1 week, Medium'. Realistically ~3-5 focused working days for a competent builder who can weld and use a lathe, plus laser-cut lead time if the parts are outsourced. The 11:21 official build video walks the entire assembly in five sub-assemblies.

  1. 1. Cut and prepare all laser parts Cut the 17 stainless parts from the DXF sets (3/5/6 mm thickness folders): 2 side plates (210x135x6), 2 bearing spacers (32x32x6), 13 knife spacers (50x50x6), 13 small fixed knives (132x25x6), 5 knife-1 / 5 knife-2 / 4 knife-3 (120x120x5), 13 big fixed knives (132x55x5), plus the 3 mm sieve/bracket parts (bearing spacer 40x40, sieve bracket 121x50, sides, top/bottom, middle). Send them to a laser cutter if you do not own one.
    Caution: Wear cut gloves and de-burr/smooth every sharp stainless edge before handling (the video explicitly smooths rough edges before tightening).
  2. 2. Build the knife/blade stack on the drive shaft Turn/fit the 27 mm hex drive shaft (L=320 mm) on the lathe. Stack the blades and spacers in the published order for 'a nice cutting flow': knife 1 (marked/demarcated) -> spacer -> knife/plate 2 (the two little dots) -> spacer -> knife/plate 3. The project invites experimenting with other blade configurations.
    Caution: The cutting head is razor-edged AISI 304 stainless - keep edges in, handle with gloves.
  3. 3. Weld the shredding box solid Weld the metal plates of the shredding box together and tighten everything so it is 'rock solid'. Mount the assembled cutting head.
    Caution: Welding PPE: eye/face protection, welding gloves, ventilation. Let welds cool before handling.
  4. 4. Run the cutting head and confirm it is silent Hand/turn the head and then run it briefly to make it operate 'completely silent' - a silent run is the alignment check that the blade stack and bearings seat correctly before proceeding.
    Caution: Keep hands and tools clear of the shaft while it is turning; run unloaded at this stage.
  5. 5. Fabricate and mount the sieve/mesh Cut perforated stainless mesh to size and weld it onto its two holders, then bolt it under the shredder. The mesh hole size sets the output flake size (finer mesh = smaller flakes but more energy and longer shred time).
    Caution: Perforated mesh edges are sharp; de-burr and wear gloves.
  6. 6. Fit the geared motor to drive the shaft Mount the motor so it drives the cutting shaft through a coupling, and align power-to-cutting perfectly (the blueprints are a reference only; alignment is the real requirement; fill any motor-size gap with a spacer). Spec floor: >=1.5 kW / 300 Nm / ~70 rpm output. The base V3 machine is a 380 V three-phase 1.5 kW; a single-phase 110/220 V 1.5 HP motor + 30:1 gearbox is a documented builder alternative. The project stresses the motor only needs to be slow and powerful.
    Caution: 380 V three-phase is a lethal supply - wiring must be done by/with a competent electrician to local code; lock out before wiring.
  7. 7. Wire the electronics with a reversible switch Install the electronics in an enclosed metal box (the video also shows a jerrycan) with a reversible switch so the motor can run forward (shred) and reverse (clear a jam). Use second-hand scrapyard cable if desired; a VFD can be used to drive the machine.
    Caution: Lethal voltage - keep wiring in an enclosed box, lock out during connection, verify no live conductors are accessible.
  8. 8. Build and mount the framework Weld a steel framework (blueprint 07); the project stresses it 'needs to be rock solid' because of the force in the machine. Mount the motor and shredding box into the frame.
    Caution: Heavy lifting and welding - use proper PPE and lifting aids for the finished machine (~90 kg).
  9. 9. Fabricate and mount the hopper Cut sheet metal (the video uses panel from an old truck) and weld the hopper together (blueprint 09); the hopper mouth is 400x200 mm. Mount it on top of the shredding box where the plastic goes in.
    Caution: Hot metal and sharp sheet edges during fabrication; wear welding and cut gloves.
  10. 10. Test run and shred Run the machine forward and reverse unloaded, then feed waste plastic (HDPE/LDPE/PP/PS, max 4 mm thick, bottle-size or smaller - pre-cut anything larger). Confirm the flake size via the mesh; reverse the switch to back out a jam.
    Caution: NEVER reach into the machine with your hands to clear a jam - use the provided push tool. Take the plug out when changing plastic type. 380 V is lethal.

Failure modes

FailureEffectMitigation
Non-uniform extrusion output (documented as the most common extrusion problem)Clogged nozzle; mixed or partially melted extrudate; unusable productCheck raw-material integrity and purity, raise the temperature, and fully empty the barrel before making a new product
Extruder jamsScrew stalls, no outputSwap two poles of the motor wiring to reverse rotation direction (documented fix)
Shredder overload - plastic resistance exceeds motor torqueMachine stops mid-run, hopper cloggedPower off, remove some of the clogged plastic, restart; feed at the documented rate
Blades do not grab the plasticFeed stalls at the hopperPush plastic toward the blades with the provided tool only (never hands); check the mesh is correct
Cross-contamination when switching plastic types (residual flake or barrel residue)Two plastic types co-melt or one obstructs the other - non-uniform output in downstream machinesStop, unplug, remove the mesh, brush out flakes (optionally blow with compressed air), clean blades and mesh, relabel output by type; empty the extruder barrel between sessions and purge 2 minutes at start

Known gaps

What the original design does not document:

  • Per-unit prices are not documented by the project: the BOM ‘Price’ column is a per-row cost, so no per-unit price is stated anywhere upstream (unit_cost is left null; row prices are in each item spec)
  • No price date, revision or other regional/currency BOM: the only BOM in the repo is the NL (European) sheet ‘BOM Netherland’
  • Bearing spec/link mismatch: the BOM item says ‘UCFL 209 45mm’ (qty 6) but the linked Conrad page slug describes a UCFL 208 40 mm flange bearing — spec must be verified before ordering
  • Motor model not documented: the motor rows link to a supplier request-form page (yr.com.tr) without an exact motor/bevel-gear model number or datasheet
  • Arduino jam-detection/auto-reverse control electronics are WIP upstream: the Schematic README’s TODO still lists ‘List of components’ and ‘Circuit diagram & PCB’ as open items (code, flowchart and wiring diagram exist)
  • The ‘laser cut ... ALL’ row does not itemize material/thickness/quantity per part; it defers to the DXF folder (58 files) without a mapping table tying each BOM part number to a plate
  • Welding and finishing consumables (welding wire, paint/coating, grinding/cutting consumables, thread locker, oil/grease) are not itemized in the BOM
  • Machine design repos (kit, shredder, extrusion, sheetpress, injection, mix) carry no root LICENSE file (GitHub license=null); the stated licence is CC-BY-SA-4.0, per the identical in-repo '0. Start/License & Disclaimer .pdf' in each (licensed by Stichting One Army).
  • The in-repo 'License & Disclaimer .pdf' is a scanned image with no embedded text layer; its CC-BY-SA-4.0 licence text was confirmed by OCR extraction, not a machine-readable LICENSE file.
  • Shredder Pro schematic sub-tree (shredder repo and kit/3. Build/5. Shredder Pro) states MIT for its code and CC-BY-SA-4.0 for its documentation (README.md), a narrower scope than the repo-wide CC-BY-SA-4.0.
  • The V2 starterkit repos (injection, mix) are Cadence-Fusion (.f3d) + STEP/IGES/SAT and bundle several machines per repo; there is no single canonical 'root assembly' file to point at, so a representative .f3d is indexed per machine sub-folder.
  • Full per-repo file index on disk carries the complete file set (kit=1629, shredder=675, extrusion=396, sheetpress=386, injection=242, mix=299, academy=595 tree entries); the pasted result carries a representative, commit-pinned subset of 71 files (one CAD sample per format, one schematic + BOM + firmware per machine family, key documents/data).
  • Academy repo (GPL-3.0) is the educational/build-instruction site, not a CAD source; it documents the machines but does not contain the build CAD itself.
  • No written step-by-step assembly instructions exist - the only procedure is the 11:21 build video plus the drawings; the academy page only carries specs and troubleshooting, not an ordered assembly sequence.
  • The Bill-of-Materials PDF's full parts table is image/vector with no extractable text layer (only 'Bearings UCFL204 20mm 2x Scrapyard' is machine-readable), so a builder must read it visually.
  • Per-sub-assembly fabrication drawings (blueprints 03-09: axe, brackets top/bottom, sieve, framework, electronics box/connector, hopper) are vector CAD with no text/notes layer, so weld sizes, hole patterns and tolerances must be read from the drawings.
  • No motor-sizing worksheet: the spec gives a floor (>=1.5 kW / 300 Nm / ~70 rpm) but the exact motor+gearbox+VFD combination and the single-phase vs three-phase choice are left to the builder and are documented on separate builder/marketplace listings, not in the core kit.
  • No electrical schematic in the core V3 kit (a schematic README exists only for the Shredder Pro variant).
  • Costs are not in the kit repo - the ~EUR 500 (new material) / ~EUR 400 (scrap material, NL) figures are published on the official academy page, the USD 1,675 is a preciousplastic-usa builder product listing (single-phase), and the '~EUR 2,200 + motor' Shredder Pro figure comes from the task's prior fit notes and was not re-verified this run.
  • The no-electricity (human-powered) variant is a separate community library hack, not part of the standard assembly path.
  • No commissioning/acceptance checklist for a finished machine - upstream documents 'run & maintain' and troubleshooting but no formal test procedure a builder follows at handover
  • No tolerance or measurement protocol on the nominal throughput figures (~10 kg/h V3, ~5 kg/h extrusion, 10-30 shots/h injection) - only the Pro's per-material values are actual measurements
  • No published extrusion parameters for EPP/expanded foam or LDPE film (melting profile, speed) - no community how-to for foam recycling was found in the retrieved sources
  • No product-quality specification for sheetpress sheets (thickness uniformity, layer bonding, flatness) beyond the 5-30 mm / 1x1 m range
  • No dimensional or strength criteria for injection moulded parts beyond 'invest in a good mould'
  • ASTM D6662-22 numeric limits are paywalled ($80) - only the scope/abstract is publicly retrievable
  • No electrical safety certification procedure (insulation resistance, RCD/GFCI testing, machine guarding standard) for the 380/400 V builds
  • No maintenance interval schedule beyond the Pro's 1-hour monitoring interval and 'check blades regularly' - the 'high in maintenance' characterization is qualitative
  • No food-contact or drinking-water safety testing path achievable by a DIY workspace (FDA case-by-case with surrogate contaminant testing and 0.5 ppb dietary-concentration limits) - drinking-water tanks remain unachievable

Safety

Buildable with controls; the 3-phase mains connection and electrical certification are the licensed-person gate. Mechanical hazards are the most severe and only partially covered: the pack gives operator rules (push tool, jam procedure, monitoring interval) and a safety-hopper option, but no guarding specification, no ISO 13857 clearances, no commissioning checklist, and no RCD in the BOM. Electrical design of the electronic box (emergency stop, 6.8 A current limiter, contactors) is sound but untested against IEC 60364; 45 bar on the hand-powered injection is below PED/AD-2015 scope; welding during build is the main process hazard with no PPE procedure in the pack; water/food use is excluded by the pack's own labeling rule, which must be enforced.

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • Flagship Shredder Pro (double shaft) is officially specified 400V three-phase, 16A, geared motor 2.2 kW minimum / 3-4 kW recommended, 1100 Nm minimum torque, 15-25 r/min, ~2200 EUR + motor, 340 kg. Measured throughput is material-dependent: PP bottle caps 41.8 kg/h, HDPE shampoo bottles 30.9 kg/h, PS CD cases 8.8 kg/h.
  • Fiji barrier confirmed in the primary source: both Think Pacific action-project documents for Precious Plastic Fiji state verbatim that the group 'lack[s] the resources/capacity to make metal moulds for our molten plastic as they are expensive to get made by a metal engineering firm'; 15-04 is explicitly a request to research which products are commercially viable for communities to sell. Machines already exist via a Field Ready partnership, so the constraint is the mould, not the machine.
  • A single-phase 110/220VAC shredder is commercially sold: PP USA / Bazar 'complete shredding machine' uses a single-phase 1.5 HP (~1.1 kW) high-torque 30:1-gearbox motor, priced USD 1,675, built to order 4-6 weeks. This is the minimum viable single-phase shredder; the V3 shredder base spec (380V, 5.8A, 1.5 kW min, ~10 kg/h, ~90 kg, ~500 EUR + motor) is the same machine class.
  • The metal-shop dependency sits in the extrusion die, per PP's own documentation: the official 'Make a mould to extrude beams' how-to lists metal tube (>=3 mm wall), metal sheet (>=3 mm), angle iron, threaded fitting, plus welding machine, angle grinder, metal saw, drill and file as required; a lasercut version is referenced. A hub with zero metal-working capacity must therefore source a die (lasercut service, a one-off local welder job, or an off-the-shelf part on the Bazar) rather than making product-shaped tooling in-house.
  • The Sheetpress is the genuinely mould-free machine in the lineup: its official workspace how-to states the outcome is 'recycled plastic sheets... up to 1x1 m and with varying thicknesses from 5mm up to 30mm', produced by a press with flat plates - no product die. Anything cut, folded or bolted from flat sheet (flat roof panels, boards, panels, non-potable tanks) avoids the metal-shop barrier entirely.
  • A true zero-electricity path is documented: a human-powered (hand-crank or pedal) shredder is an official Precious Plastic community library build, explicitly recommended when access to electricity is difficult (off-grid). The community also notes the shredder motor can drive the extrusion screw to save energy, and that only the extruder heat bands (~600 W) would still need power in a manual-drive setup.
  • PALLETS: feasible without a metal workshop. Pallet Design documents plastic-lumber pallets 'assembled from extruded plastic boards... in the same stringer or block layouts as a wooden pallet and fastened with screws or nails. It needs no mold, so any size is possible in small quantities', with ISPM 15 exemption and rot-proofing as advantages; deckboards are typically 19 mm HDPE. Field validation: Brothers Make (2022) extruded beams from shredded DVD cases on a Precious Plastic extruder and assembled a working pallet ('once we'd assembled it all it was pretty rock solid'), noting each slat is replaceable. Caveat: their beams came from a beam die, so the die is the only metal step and can be a one-off welder/lasercut job; a fully mould-free variant uses sheetpress sheet cut to boards.
  • PLASTIC LUMBER: feasible and a real standardised category. ASTM D6662-22 is a full performance specification for polyolefin-based plastic lumber decking boards 'currently made predominantly with recycled polyolefin plastics (in particular high-density polyethylene)', covering flexural properties, dimensional stability, weatherability, fire and slip resistance, plus a span-length calculation procedure - giving a hub a concrete target standard for extruded or sheet-cut boards (typical sections per Pallet Design: 19 mm deckboards, 38x89 mm stringers).
