The need

Households below the World Bank extreme-poverty line and in acute multidimensional poverty, concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and fragile/conflict-affected states, with large rural shares. 831 million people in extreme poverty at <US$3.00/day (2021 PPP), down from ~2.3 billion in 1990; 412 million children (<=17) are in such households (2024). At <US$2.15/day almost 700 million (8.5% of the world) live in extreme poverty; 3.5 billion (44%) are poor at the US$6.85/day upper-middle-income line. 1.1 billion of 6.3 billion (109 countries) are in acute multidimensional poverty, over half children, with common deprivations of no clean cooking fuel, housing, sanitation, nutrition and electricity. 645 million faced hunger in 2025 (7.8%); Africa now holds ~309 million hungry people, overtaking Asia (~292 million). SSA is 16% of world population but 67% of the extreme poor (three-quarters including all fragile/conflict-affected countries); 72% of the extreme poor live in IDA-eligible countries.

Chronic, decades-long, and currently worsening in Africa and conflict zones, with a documented overlap with climate hazards. Poverty reduction has slowed to a near standstill: 622 million projected in extreme poverty in 2030; eradicating it 'could take decades' and lifting people above US$6.85/day 'more than a century'. Only ~69 million are projected to escape extreme poverty in 2024-2030 vs ~150 million in 2013-2019. Climate overlap: of the 1.1 billion in multidimensional poverty, 887 million are exposed to at least one of four hazards (high heat, drought, floods, air pollution), and 309 million face three or four concurrent hazards; one in five people is at risk of an extreme weather event in their lifetime. Food access is deteriorating: world food prices rose 5.8% y/y in September 2026 (FAO Food Price Index 136.0), cereal prices 17.2% above the same month a year earlier.

What people do now

The dominant response is to cut back food and spend, and to fall back on low-quality energy, trading off nutrition and health. Food-insecure households cope by worrying about running out of food, eating less than sufficient, and skipping meals or going a day without food because of lack of money (FAO FIES). A healthy diet costs US$4.22 PPP per person per day, above the ~US$3.00 PPP extreme-poverty line; 2.69 billion people (nearly one in three) cannot afford a healthy diet, Africa furthest behind. Cooking energy is a recurring cost and health burden: fewer than half the world still cook with solid fuels (wood, dung, charcoal, coal), strongly tied to low income, causing pneumonia, stroke, heart disease, COPD and lung cancer. The recurring-cost cushion is thin: SDG 1.3 targets substantial coverage of the poor and vulnerable by 2030, and the current gap is the missing safety net; well-designed programs cost only ~1.0-1.5% of GDP, so the coverage gap is a political/financial, not technical, limit.

Where existing solutions fall short

Scale of the recurring-cost cushion: universal social-protection coverage (SDG 1.3) is far from met, leaving most of the 645 million facing hunger and 1.1 billion in multidimensional poverty without a shock-absorbing safety net. Affordability of essentials: 2.69 billion cannot afford a healthy diet even where food is available - a structural affordability gap, not just a production gap. Energy access: fewer than half the world cook with clean fuels and a large share lack electricity, concentrated in rural poor households - clean cooking and off-grid power remain unsolved at scale. Pace of progress: even well-executed programs are out-run by population growth and climate shocks (food prices +5.8% y/y; 887 million poor exposed to hazards), so incremental top-down fixes do not close the gap.

What a solution must do

  • Reduce the food/energy cost burden of a target household by a quantifiable amount, e.g. lower the per-person cost of an adequate diet or of clean cooking fuel toward the US$3.00 PPP/day threshold against the US$4.22 PPP/day healthy-diet benchmark (FAO SOFI 2026)
  • Improve a concrete, named nutrition or energy-access indicator, e.g. reduce the share cooking with solid fuels, or raise per-capita energy to at least the IEA rural-access minimum of 250 kWh/year
  • Increase measured livelihood income or reduce shock exposure, verifiable against a baseline, e.g. a reduction in FAO FIES severity or in the share of the target population exposed to a named climate hazard
  • Be affordable in materials/operations to a low-income household: incremental cost well below a poverty-line income (ideally low double-digit US$ per unit) so it does not itself become a recurring cost
  • Be maintainable with local skills, materials and climate conditions and operate offline / low-connectivity (see constraints)
  • Be safe: if an energy solution, avoid solid-fuel indoor pollution, since the MPI flags 'no clean cooking fuel' as a core deprivation

Constraints

  • Power: a large share of the poor lack reliable electricity; IEA rural access minimum is 250 kWh/year and urban 500 kWh/year, so solutions must run at/near that level or be grid-independent (solar/off-grid)
  • Tools/skills/materials: must be buildable and repairable by local hands with locally available materials; low-electricity, solid-fuel settings constrain what can be powered or maintained
  • Climate: target settings face high heat, drought, floods and air pollution (887 million poor exposed to >=1 of these), so solutions must tolerate these
  • Connectivity: rural poor settings have limited connectivity (tied to low income and lack of electricity/assets) - assume offline operation and low bandwidth
  • Recurring cost: the binding constraint is affordability - food and energy are already the largest household spending, so any solution must not add a recurring cost a <US$3/day household cannot absorb
