Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS)

From Applied Commons, the open catalogue of ways to meet human needs
Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS)
NeedCold chain and storage
ServesFood and nutrition, Economic and livelihood security, Environmental stability and resource security
StatusBuild pack ready
LicenceNone found
Climatestropical, arid, temperate, cold
Parts costabout €1.65 (1.80 USD)
SafetyNeeds a qualified person
The safety review found that parts of this build need a qualified person.

Hermetic grain storage: a cost-effective triple-layer bag (two HDPE inner layers + woven polypropylene outer) that stores shelled grain (maize, sorghum, wheat, rice, etc.) 6-12 months without fumigation, at ~$2.50 per 50 kg. Gates-funded PICS3 is field-deployed across West and East Africa; public Purdue extension PDFs document the design. Field-proven, low-cost, locally sourced - the direct match to the airtight/dry grain requirement. Requires shelled grain and closing labour, a known adoption constraint the brief notes.

Assembly

Tools: Cord (string twine), to twist, fold and tie each liner lip and the outer sack - Kharel 2018 describes the conventional method as 'twisting, folding, and tying the lip of each layer individually with a cord', Tape, to patch small holes or tears in the HDPE liners and maintain airtightness (Purdue Extension Bulletin E-264), Clean hands and a clean work surface - E-264: store clean grain containing no debris (which tends to make holes in the bags), and handle the bags carefully, especially when tying and transporting them, A scale, to fill to the bag's rated capacity (bags come in 25, 50 and 100 kg capacities per IFT) - inferred practical tool; no retrieved source explicitly requires weighing the fill, Optional: a hand moisture meter, to check grain moisture before sealing (no retrieved source gives a specific target value)
Skills: Twist-fold-and-tie the plastic liner lips by hand so each layer is airtight (the core manual labour; the time-critical step), Assess grain dryness before storage (the bag preserves, it does not dry, the grain), Inspect and tape small holes/tears to keep the bag hermetic, Choose and maintain a clean, rodent-free, shaded storage location
Time: Estimated 25-75 minutes per 50 kg unit, end to end. Only the sealing step is directly measured: in a peer-reviewed trial on 50 kg maize bags (Kharel 2018) it ran 46.78 s (FT: both liners folded together and tied), 57.87 s (IR), 72.50 s (TT: conventional twist-tie), 73.25 s (FS), with FT >34% faster than TT. No retrieved source times the fill, so most of the 25-75 min window is the manual filling of ~50 kg of shelled grain, which is an estimate, not a measurement.

  1. Dry the grain to a safe moisture level and clean it of debris Thoroughly dry the harvested grain before storage and remove debris, which tends to puncture the bags. The retrieved sources do not state a numeric target for long-term storage, but the short-term-drying ceiling is explicit: maize can be held in a PICS bag at <=21% moisture for 21 days with minimal germination loss and no visible mold; at 24% moisture, germination fell 95.5 percentage points and a fermentation smell appeared after 21 days.
    Caution: Grain must be thoroughly dried prior to storage to prevent mold growth and grain rot; a sealed wet bag can also generate a fermentation smell and loss of germination. (Quality gate; the 21% figure is a 21-day short-term drying ceiling, not a long-term storage spec.)
  2. Fill the first HDPE liner Place the clean, dried, shelled grain into the first 80-micron HDPE inner liner (a 50 kg or 100 kg capacity bag; 25 kg is also sold). Weigh to the bag's rated capacity. The liner, not the woven sack, is the airtight layer - the woven polypropylene provides only mechanical strength.
    Caution: Handle the bag gently; avoid damaging the liners, especially when tying and transporting (E-264). Overfilling can tear the thin 80-micron film.
  3. Seal the first liner airtight Seal the first liner tightly so no air can enter, using the conventional method: twist, fold, and tie the lip of the liner individually with a cord. This is the most time-consuming step; a faster, tested method is to fold the liner lip over and tie it (see the note on FT below).
    Caution: The seal must be airtight - the hermetic (oxygen-depleting) effect only works if the liner is fully sealed. A loose seal lets oxygen in and defeats the technology.
  4. Note: faster 'FT' closure (cited alternative, optional) A peer-reviewed time-saving method (Kharel et al. 2018, 50 kg maize bags) reduces bag sealing time by >34% versus the per-layer twist-tie: 'FT' = both liners folded together and tied (46.78 s for a 50 kg bag vs 72.50 s for TT). Other tested methods: IR (inner liner rolled onto itself, middle liner fold-tied) saved ~20%; FS (both liners folded and tied separately) was slower (73.25 s). 'FT' means FOLDED-together-and-tied - it is not a foot-twist; no retrieved source describes closing the bag with the foot.
    Caution: Any method is acceptable as long as the final seal is airtight; the study found all tested closure methods maintained low oxygen levels similar to the conventional twist-tie over the storage period, so a faster tie does not compromise hermeticity if it seals.
  5. Nest and seal the second HDPE liner Surround the first (sealed) polyethylene bag with a second, identical HDPE liner and seal/tie that one airtight as well, making the double-bag combination airtight. This is the 'triple-layer' composite: two HDPE liners plus one woven PP outer sack.
    Caution: The two-liner redundancy is what lets the bag keep functioning after handling and minor damage (E-264); do not skip the second liner.
  6. Place the double-bag in the woven polypropylene outer sack and close it Put the sealed double-bag into the woven polypropylene (PP) outer sack, which provides the mechanical strength of the PICS bag, and tie or fold-and-tie the top of the outer sack to close the unit. In the conventional twist-tie method the outer woven bag is twist-tied 'to provide firm support to the bagging system' (Kharel 2018). The PP sack is not the airtight layer - the airtightness comes from the two HDPE liners already sealed.
    Caution: Handle carefully during transport; the outer sack protects the fragile liners. Keep the bag's opening dry and clean.
  7. Inspect the finished unit and patch any small holes or tears Check the assembled bag for small holes or tears and patch them with tape to maintain airtightness (E-264). Recycle the bag for other uses only when the liners have accumulated too many holes/tears to repair with tape.
    Caution: A compromised seal reintroduces oxygen and the technology stops working. Clean storage keeps the bag free of rodent damage.
  8. Store the sealed bag in a clean, rodent-free, shaded location Store the sealed, filled bag in a clean area to avoid damage by rodents and other household objects, away from direct sunlight and extreme heat (E-264). On low-resource farms filled bags are commonly stored in living or sleeping rooms and are kept (not sold with the grain) for reuse the following storage season (E-264).
    Caution: PICS bags do not provide a barrier against mice, rats, or other larger animals (Wikipedia, citing the Purdue Technician Training Manual) - so the storage location, not the bag, must protect against them. Avoid direct sunlight and extreme heat to protect the plastic.

Known gaps

What the original design does not document:

  • No per-part engineering BOM is published upstream (exact film area/weight per 50 kg liner, part numbers, closure-tie spec and count) - component-level unit costs left null rather than guessed
  • Component-level per-part USD costs in West/East Africa not re-verified this run; the only historical figure (Mali 2010: ~500 FCFA liner pair, ~51% of factory price; factory delivery 925 FCFA) is from a prior run and not re-cited
  • No current (2025-26) single-bag in-country retail price was retrieved; the 2012 Burkina Faso price and the 2026 USD 2-3 largest-size estimate are the closest anchors
  • Exact moisture threshold and 6-12 month storage claim are stated across sources but the per-crop duration was not isolated from a single source this run
  • No CAD / parametric model, blueprint, or fabrication repository exists for the PICS hermetic bag — confirmed by GitHub repo search (0 results across 'pics hermetic grain storage', 'pics bag storage', 'hermetic grain bag', 'purdue improved crop storage', and sheller variants; no Purdue org hosts a PICS design repo). The bag must be fabricated to spec from the extension PDFs; a builder wanting a parametric model must obtain it from Purdue Cal's directly or draft their own from the dimensioned pages.
  • No standalone dimensioned drawing file exists: the full bag-construction geometry (triple-layer layering, 50/100 kg sizes, lip-and-knot sealing) is carried only inside the E-265-W PDF and the PICS3 poster, not as a separate CAD/drawing file.
  • Exact author/title of the 2018 JSPR efficacy paper (PII S0022474X18301371) could not be fully verified: ScienceDirect and the publisher landing page bot-blocked metadata extraction, so it is indexed by journal + year + PII + host folder rather than full author/title; confirm against the first page before relying on it for the efficacy numbers.
  • Hand-maize-sheller cut-list provenance: the dimensioned cut list is attributed to Appropedia / Engineers Without Borders (appropedia.org); that upstream source should be confirmed before mass reproduction if exact tolerances matter (18-22 mm drum-housing clearance).
  • PICS is ambient hermetic storage with no temperature control or monitoring, so no design for a monitoring sub-system is in scope; if the broader build-pack assumes a sensor/monitoring component, that is outside the PICS bag as designed (health cold-chain fit = MISSED per the input fit brief).
  • Exact cord material, length, and number of ties per layer are not specified in any retrieved source (Kharel 2018 says only 'with a cord'; E-264 says only 'when tying').
  • No numeric target moisture content for LONG-TERM storage; only a <=21%-for-21-days ceiling for the short-term wet-maize drying scenario (Yewle 2024). The sources say 'thoroughly dried' but give no figure.
  • No fill-level / how-full guidance (partial vs full), and no stated maximum fill height relative to the tying area.
  • No explicit instruction to store the bag off the ground, away from walls, or on a pallet - only 'clean area, away from direct sunlight and extreme heat' (E-264).
  • The 'Purdue Improved Cowpea Storage - Technician Training Manual' (2008), cited as ref [5] on the Wikipedia page, is NOT among the retrieved sources, so the fine-grained hand motions of the conventional tie rely on the peer-reviewed paper's description plus the Wikipedia use summary.
  • Source scope: the measured sealing times are for 50 kg maize bags specifically (Kharel 2018); 25 kg and 100 kg timings are not separately reported.
  • No guidance on whether to pre-inspect the empty bag for pre-existing liner defects before filling.
  • No published bag-level hermeticity / airtightness test standard (ASABE / ISO / CIGR) specific to PICS bags was found in the retrieved sources; published protocols are ad-hoc O2-indicator (Oxysense/Oxydot) and USB data-logger methods, so a standardized O2 pass/fail threshold for the bag itself is undefined
  • Field (smallholder) validation of the full 6-12 month MAIZE window is thinner than the cowpea window - most published efficacy is cowpea plus medium/short-term maize lab studies; the 6-12 month maize claim leans on the need brief and the 692-household Nepal RCT
  • The ~10 kg/50-kg-bag (~1/3 baseline) loss-reduction figure is the need brief's causal estimate (Nepal RCT, 692 households, ~44 kg/household/season baseline); the full RCT paper body was not fully extracted here, so its exact estimator/CI could not be independently re-derived
  • No standardized aflatoxin / mold acceptance threshold tied to PICS was located in the retrieved sources
  • Rodent protection is out of scope for the bag unit itself (structure-level test) and is not documented for PICS
  • Reuse limit (>=3 seasons) is extension/industry guidance, not a documented pass/fail durability test

Safety

Safe with the controls below. No qualified-person step is required (no mains, gas, pressure vessel, or load-bearing construction in scope) and nothing in the design is unsafe as built. Field trial and home deployment require: (1) an enforced moisture gate before sealing and aflatoxin/mold checks at open, (2) a mechanical-safety instruction for the hand sheller, (3) a rodent-proof storage structure, and (4) confirmation that liner film and patching tape are food-grade.

HazardSeverityControls
Aflatoxin and mold growth in grain sealed at too-high moisture. The hermetic bag holds (does not lower) grain moisture, so wet grain in a sealed bag can develop mold, fermentation odor, and aflatoxin over the 6-12 month storage window; the pack's own high-moisture trial measured ~95.5-percentage-point germination loss and visible mold at 24% m.c. after 21 days, and aflatoxin is a liver carcinogen with no species known to be immune and children especially vulnerable.highSeal grain only at <=13-14% m.c. (confirm by moisture reading at fill; the bag is not a dryer); Use the 21% m.c. / 21-day ceiling only as a short drying hold, never as a 6-12 month storage spec; Minimize shelling/kernel-break damage (broken kernels carry higher aflatoxin risk); At open: test for aflatoxin (ELISA/immunoassay or lab HPLC), check for visible mold and fermentation smell, discard damaged kernels before food use
Hand maize sheller (the required pre-step: the bag stores shelled grain). A hand sheller uses a rotating drum/cone to force cobs between the shelling surfaces - a pinch and laceration hazard to hands/loose clothing if cobs are fed in too fast, the drum is unguarded, or a clog is cleared by hand while it is turning.mediumGuarantee the sheller drum is stopped and fully stationary before clearing a clog or reaching in; Keep hands clear of the feed throat; use a push-stick rather than fingers to feed cobs; Tie back or remove loose clothing and secure hair near the rotating parts; Add an explicit sheller safety warning to the build pack (currently absent from the safety section)
Manual handling of full bags: a 50 kg bag is ~50 kg of dense grain (100 kg size worse); lifting, carrying, and stacking filled bags without a pallet/floor can cause low-back strain, dropped-load crush, or a bag tearing and spilling. Stacking heavy bags also risks a topple on a person.mediumLift with the legs, keep the load close, and use two people for 100 kg bags; Store on a flat, level floor or pallet (not on the ground in a way that invites water ingress) and stack no higher than one or two bags high, square-stacked; Do not stack a full bag on top of a person or a leaning bag
Rodent (mouse/rat) intrusion causes total or near-total loss of the stored grain. PICS bags give NO rodent barrier - the protection must come from the storage structure, and the bag can be gnawed open if set on the floor in a rodent-prone location.mediumStore raised off the floor on pallets/shelves, away from walls, in a sealed rodent-proof structure; Add physical rodent control (traps/bait at the structure perimeter) and keep the storage point free of other attractants; Move the rodent-protection requirement to the storage-structure spec, not the bag (currently a failure-mode note, not a control)
Food-contact material safety of the liners and patching tape. The grain is in direct contact with two 80-micron HDPE liners for up to 12 months, and small liner holes are patched with adhesive tape that also contacts food; migration of plasticizers or adhesive constituents into the stored grain is not addressed in the pack.lowConfirm the HDPE liner film is food-grade (HDPE is widely used food-contact packaging); Confirm the tape used for patching is a food-safe adhesive, or patch with an inner (non-food-facing) film rather than tape on the food side; Keep the bag's opening and interior dry and free of foreign matter
Co-storage with fumigated or pesticide-treated grain. PICS is a chemical-free hermetic method; if grain that was fumigated or sprayed is sealed in a bag, residual chemical could be trapped against the grain and carried through to food use.lowStore only grain that has not been fumigated or chemically treated (core PICS design feature); Do not co-store with fumigated grain or grain carrying pesticide residue
Direct sunlight and extreme heat degrade the thin 80-micron HDPE film and the woven PP sack (embrittlement, loss of strength, seal failure), which then defeats hermeticity. There is no active fire source, so this is a material-degradation risk, not a fire risk.lowStore in a shaded, cool location away from direct sunlight and heat sources (already a pack step); Inspect liners for embrittlement or cracking at the start of each season and retire a bag that fails the O2/airtightness check

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • Yewle 2024 (Foods 13(5):760) is a controlled growth-chamber study (25 C, 80% RH, 21 days), not a field trial: PICS bags preserved 18% and 21% m.c. maize at constant moisture with no visible mold (O2 fell below 1%), with germination losses of 0.5 and 6.2 percentage points, while 24% m.c. maize failed even in hermetic bags (95.5 pp germination loss, lactic-acid fermentation, pH drop); the authors' own verdict is that <=21% m.c. for 21 days is the safe boundary and 'field testing is required' before farm deployment.
  • The closing step of the conventional PICS triple-bag is explicitly documented by its own developers as a labour burden: bags are closed by twisting, folding and tying the lip of each of the three layers individually with cord, which is 'not only time and labor intensive, but also may affect the integrity of the liners' (Kharel et al. 2018, J Stored Prod Res).
  • A redesigned closure demonstrably cuts sealing time: in a controlled comparison, fold-and-tie (both liners folded together and tied) was the most time-saving method, reducing 50 kg PICS bag sealing time by more than 34% versus the conventional twist-tie method, with no difference in oxygen-depletion rate or pest suppression (Kharel et al. 2018).
  • Scope/verification caveat: the cost, willingness-to-pay and VAT/demand evidence is from Tanzania (AGRA 2019), while the causal loss-reduction is from Uganda (Omotilewa et al. 2018 RCT, re-analyzed in JSSF 2026), so this is a cross-country synthesis; the JSSF 2026 paper is a single-author secondary IV re-analysis rather than the primary field study; and neither source reports a standalone measured effect of point-of-sale micro-credit at scale. The Omotilewa et al. 2018 AJAE paper (the primary RCT) was not directly retrieved and is cited here as referenced in source [1].
  • Field validation for PICS-class hermetic bags exists only for dry-season pest storage of dry grain: a 4-cycle on-farm trial in the Mexican highlands (Odjo & Verhulst 2024, Field Crops Research) showed hermetic bags from three manufacturers kept moisture below 13%, insect damage below 5% and weight loss below 2% even after insect perforation and tape repair, over three-month cycles; a Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems 2022 analysis based on field-trial data quantified insect-loss reduction and GHG benefits for dry maize storage. Neither covers grain at 18-24% m.c.
  • The 2023 companion study (Yewle et al., Foods 12(4):891, same group) is a 21-day jar experiment giving the same qualitative result (18% m.c. ok ~14 days, 21% ok ~7 days, 24% fails) and explicitly calls for farm-level research 'on farms and along the grain value chain'; together the two lab studies bound the proposed safe operating window but provide no field or farm-scale evidence.
  • The burden scales to tonne-level: a 2025 CGIAR AICCRA brief (Zambia) states 'hand shelling can take about 14 labour-days to shell a ton of maize, the machine can do that in less than an hour', positioning the mechanised sheller as a low-cost labour-saving option for smallholders.
  • A low-cost mechanical shelling aid is established and cheap: a hand-operated maize sheller fabricated in Nigeria cost about $61 (NGN 27,100 at 1 USD = 447.52 NGN) and delivered 12-60 kg/h at 40-120 rpm, shelling efficiency 94-100%, kernel damage 0.12-0.95% (Okusanya & Agbongiaban 2023).
