The need

Rural and remote low-income households, informal-market smallholders and last-mile service providers in low- and lower-middle-income countries - chiefly sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and the least developed countries. About 1 billion people live in rural areas isolated by distance, terrain and transport poverty; 70% of the rural population in Africa lack all-season road and transport access; 50-60% of people in poor countries live more than 8 km from a healthcare facility. Ethiopia's Rural Connectivity for Food Security Program targets 11.3 million rural people (50% women) and notes food insecurity among over 20 million from poor road connectivity. Roughly 3 billion vaccine doses a year (UNICEF) must reach the last mile; and the 1.19 million annual road-traffic deaths concentrate among pedestrians, cyclists and motorcyclists in low- and middle-income countries.

Severe and persistent. Road safety has barely moved: 1.19 million deaths a year in 2021, the leading cause of death for 5-29 year-olds, more than half among vulnerable road users in LMICs; halving by 2030 is still off track. Sub-Saharan Africa loses ~23% of food in post-harvest processes, Western Africa ~27%, LDCs ~20%, versus a 13.3% world average - losses stable from 2015 to 2023, not falling. Transport poverty is structural, not transient: 70% of rural Africa lacks all-season roads, 50-60% live >8 km from care, and exporting from a low-income country is 57% costlier than from a high-income one. The last mile is where the cold chain breaks, where food spoils, and where people die.

What people do now

People walk, head-load, animal-draw (donkey, camel) or use expensive informal transport. In rural areas of developing countries good road connectivity is the exception; the World Bank notes 27% of chronically poor Kenyan farm households live at least 11.5 km from a tarmac road. UNICEF's vaccine cold chain ends in cold boxes carried by car, motorcycle, bicycle, donkey, camel or on foot to the village. Poor road access and limited cold chains in sub-Saharan Africa mean a significant share of crops spoils before reaching markets, eroding farmer incomes and pushing up food prices. Transport cost is the price of distance: cutting a typical food shipment in LMICs by 100 km lowers cost ~20%, using highways instead of smaller roads cuts ~19%, and the rainy season adds ~6% more - so the same load costs far more to move, and often not at all, than the distance itself would imply. UNICEF reports that shifting one vaccine shipment from air to sea could cut GHG emissions by ~90% and freight costs by ~50% - a sign that the current modal mix is not the cheapest.

Where existing solutions fall short

Existing solutions are expensive, fuel- or grid-dependent, and hard to repair locally. Cold rooms, refrigerated trucks and modern vaccine carriers exist but are priced and powered for the formal economy; UNICEF itself notes that even its 3-billion-dose-a-year supply chain ends in donkey and foot. The CTC framework that would let many vaccines travel without a full cold chain is only at pilot stage for a few vaccines. Hermetic grain storage and solar cold rooms are proven but remain unevenly adopted in sub-Saharan Africa - FAO's 2025-2026 program explicitly cites 'uptake remains uneven'. There is no open, locally buildable, cheap last-mile vehicle or carrier that matches the informal baseline (walking, head-load, animal-draw) on cost-per-km, and no open logistics tool that works on a basic phone without continuous connectivity. The World Bank's own evidence - that a 100 km distance cut saves ~20% of a food shipment's cost - points to the missing piece: a carrier or network that effectively shrinks the economic distance for the poorest 1 billion, not a bigger road or a fancier truck.

What a solution must do

  • Food and nutrition (SDG 2): a carrier or storage solution must keep a household's typical perishable harvest (e.g. 100-200 kg of grain or fruit) safe for at least 2-4 weeks after harvest, cutting the ~23% sub-Saharan post-harvest loss; and must cost under ~5% of the gross value of the cargo it protects over its life.
  • Health and safety (SDG 3): a vaccine or cold-storage solution must hold the long-term +2 to +8 degC band for at least 48 hours unattended at the last-mile point, or the short-term controlled-temperature-chain profile of at least +40 degC for at least 3 days as WHO CTC criteria require; and must deliver a standard district cold-box payload (e.g. 50-100 doses of a routine immunization vaccine) without refrigerated re-cooling.
  • Mobility and safety (SDG 11.2): a low-cost vehicle must carry a loaded adult plus a 50-100 kg payload on a typical all-season rural road or track, at a speed that makes the 8-km trip to a clinic feasible within a 2-hour daily window, and must be operable and repairable with parts available within a 50 km radius.
  • Mobility (SDG 11.2): a logistics solution must cut door-to-door travel time for a typical rural-urban round trip by at least 40-50%, matching the World Bank's observed 48% reduction and 2x job accessibility from better connectivity; and must be affordable at under ~10% of a low-income household's monthly income for a standard monthly trip.
  • Water and sanitation (SDG 6): a last-mile water or WASH-supplies delivery must carry at least 100-200 litres of clean water (or an equivalent WASH kit serving a 10-person household for a week) to a point of use within a 10 km radius, at a cost under US$0.50 per 100 litres per trip over the asset's life.
  • Sanitation and shelter (SDG 6/11): a supply-chain tool (open source, local) must track a single consignment - from a district warehouse to the last point of use - with at most 3 status updates a day on a basic mobile phone, without requiring continuous connectivity, and must be usable by a 15-year-old after 1 hour of training.
  • Safety (SDG 3): a vehicle or road-asset solution must reduce the fatality rate among vulnerable road users (pedestrian, cyclist, motorcycle) in the areas it serves by at least 20% within 3 years of deployment, consistent with WHO's target of halving road-traffic deaths by 2030.

Constraints

  • Power is unreliable or absent at the last mile - solutions must work offline, on solar, or on a small battery, and must not depend on a stable grid connection.
  • Fuel, lubricant and spare-parts supply chains are thin in remote areas; a solution must be repairable with parts available within ~50 km and must tolerate the absence of a dedicated mechanic.
  • Roads are often unpaved, seasonal and impassable in the rainy season (rainy-season transport cost is ~6% higher); the asset must handle a loaded 40-80 km/day on a typical rural track, not just a paved road.
  • Operating cost per unit moved must beat the informal alternative (walking, head-load, animal-draw, motorcycle taxi) or the solution will not be used - the World Bank's own evidence is that cutting distance by 100 km cuts cost ~20%, so any carrier must be at least that much cheaper per km.
  • Maintenance, calibration and spare parts must be locally available and cheaply replaceable; the solution must be buildable from parts a local workshop can source or machine.
  • Safety is non-negotiable: a solution that moves people must not increase the fatality rate for vulnerable road users; WHO's data show more than half of LMIC road deaths are among pedestrians, cyclists and motorcyclists.
  • The solution must be open (designs, BOM, firmware) so communities, NGOs and local manufacturers can adapt and scale it without a single vendor; the World Bank's LPI 2.0 notes that the supply-chain data ecosystem is still maturing, so the tooling must work with minimal external connectivity.
