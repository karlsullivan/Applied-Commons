CoolVeg (MIT D-Lab spinout)
NeedFood and nutrition

LicenceNone found
Climatestropical, arid, temperate
Parts costabout €983 (1,100 USD)

Non-profit (2022, spun out of MIT D-Lab) publishing open-source evaporative-cooling designs - clay-pot coolers and a forced-air evaporative cooling chamber - to extend fruit and vegetable shelf life in hot, dry, off-grid climates, plus a dissemination training program. Helps off-grid smallholders reduce post-harvest loss. Maturity: field-tested - research in 6 countries across Africa and South Asia; design documentation publicly detailed.

Assembly

Tools: Welding set-up (MIG or stick) plus grinder/cut-off, for the steel racks and the exhaust-channel sheet, Metal cutting: chop-saw, tin snips, or hacksaw (for the 50 mm insulation sandwich panels and the aluminium/galvanized exhaust-channel sheet), Measuring and layout: tape measure, square, marker, Drill/driver with appropriate bits and screws, Caulk gun with a wet/humid-environment sealant (report prefers caulk over duct tape for panel junctions), Airflow meter / anemometer (report recommends one for checking airflow at the crates), If off-grid: basic electrical wiring tools, multimeter, and a correctly rated fuse/breaker for the solar + battery system
Skills: Simple metalworking: cutting and welding (crate rack, ground/stand rack, exhaust-channel sheet) — CoolVeg report p.26, Basic electrical knowledge — only if installing a solar PV + battery power system — CoolVeg report p.26, General: cutting/fitting insulation sandwich panels, applying sealant, fitting door hinges and a weather-strip gasket, installing mesh screens
Time: Two people, 2-3 days (CoolVeg report p.26: 'A portable chamber capable of storing 500 kgs of produce can be constructed by two people in 2-3 days')

  1. 1. Source and stage the materials (bill of materials) Order/collect per the report's materials list (p.23): (a) one off-the-shelf evaporative cooler — target listed airflow 4,000-8,000 CMH (2,300-4,700 CFM), 150-300 W for a 25-crate chamber (p.21); (b) 50 mm insulation sandwich panels (EPS/XPS/PUF), clad aluminium or galvanized steel, white/reflective (thinner panels not recommended), cut to the Nigeria 25-crate table (p.65): bottom & top 81x212x5 cm, rear 202x180x5, left & right doors 106x210x5, left & right sides 81x200x5; (c) a rear exhaust-channel metal sheet 212x160 cm x 0.08 in aluminium or galvanized steel (p.65); (d) right-angle brackets for the panel junctions and hinges for the two doors; (e) 40 mm galvanized/polymer-coated/painted steel square tube for the interior crate rack and the ground/stand rack; (f) a wet-environment sealant (caulk, not duct tape); (g) a door weather-strip or refrigerator gasket; (h) 1-2 mm mesh screens plus coarser chicken-wire support. Crate size 60x40x23 cm (p.24).
    Caution: Handle cut metal and panel edges with cut-resistant gloves; ventilate the area when using sealants.
  2. 2. Build the ground/stand rack to lift the chamber off the ground Fabricate and weld a frame from 40 mm galvanized/polymer-coated/painted steel square tube (p.23) sized to the chamber footprint so the finished insulated box sits off the ground, protecting the insulation panels and giving clearance. This is the chamber's base.
    Caution: Welding fumes — work in a ventilated area with a fume mask and eye/ear protection; let welds cool before handling.
  3. 3. Build the interior crate rack Fabricate and weld a second frame from 40 mm steel square tube (p.23) sized to hold the crate stacks (five stacks of five crates for the 25-crate layout, p.21). The report requires the rack to keep the crates at least 20 cm above the chamber floor (p.22).
    Caution: Same welding PPE as step 2; verify rack strength before loading crates.
  4. 4. Assemble the insulated box from the panels Join the seven insulation sandwich panels (bottom, top, rear, left side, right side, plus the two door panels) into a sealed box (p.65). Fasten the panel junctions with the right-angle brackets and seal every junction with the wet-environment sealant (p.23). Keep the crate-to-wall gaps small — less than 1 cm in each direction; gaps over 2 cm let a significant portion of air bypass the crates (p.22; the report's example interior: 61 cm front-to-back for a 60 cm crate, 202 cm interior width for five 40 cm crates).
    Caution: Sharp panel edges — gloves; keep the interior clear of sealant that could contaminate stored produce once cured.
  5. 5. Fit the doors and seal the door gap Hinge the two door panels onto the box and apply the door weather-strip / refrigerator gasket around the door perimeter. The adhesive used must withstand getting wet and repeated opening and closing of the doors (p.23).
    Caution: None specific.
  6. 6. Form and fit the rear exhaust channel Secure the 212x160 cm aluminium/galvanized metal sheet (p.65) to the rear of the chamber to create an exhaust channel that is at least 15 cm deep and runs the full length of the chamber (~200 cm, ~0.3 m2 cross-section) (p.22). The cutout at the bottom of the rear panel should start at least 20 cm above the chamber floor so it lines up with the crate rack (p.22), and the exhaust outlet must be at least 30 cm below the ceiling to allow a screen to fit without restricting airflow (p.22).
    Caution: Metalwork PPE (cut/eye) when cutting and fitting the sheet.
  7. 7. Mount the evaporative cooler on a side panel Cut a hole in the left or right side panel that is LARGER than the cooler's outlet — the report warns against any blockage of the air exiting the cooler — and mount the cooler so its fan blows directly into the chamber (p.21).
    Caution: Cut PPE; deburr the hole so there are no sharp edges where hands or produce pass.
  8. 8. Install intake/exhaust screens Fit 1-2 mm metal or plastic mesh screens over the exhaust outlet and the cooler's air inlet, backed by a coarser chicken-wire support. The opening supporting each screen should be oversized relative to the air outlet channel so the screen does not restrict airflow (p.23).
    Caution: None specific.
  9. 9. Set up the airflow path through the crates Route the cool, humid air from the cooler so it is directed downward onto and through the stacks of crates, exits the bottom of the crates, rises up the rear exhaust channel, and leaves through the top exhaust outlet (p.21, Figure 6). Check that the total cross-sectional area of the crate-bottom openings is more than 50% of the crate-bottom area, and that gaps between crates/walls stay under ~1 cm, so the crates (not another restriction) dominate the airflow resistance (p.22).
    Caution: Keep hands and loose clothing clear of the fan.
  10. 10. (Off-grid only) Add and wire the solar + battery power system For off-grid use, install solar PV panels, battery storage, an inverter, and a charge controller to power the evaporative cooler, and wire them in. This is the step that requires the report's 'basic electrical knowledge, if installing a solar + battery power system' (p.26). The report lists component lifetimes (panels 25-30 yr, batteries 3-10 yr, inverters 10-15 yr, charge controllers 5-15 yr; p.52) and includes the off-grid power system in its off-grid cost figures (e.g., Nigeria 25-crate $1,300 on-grid / $2,300 off-grid; p.51), but gives no per-chamber panel/battery sizing.
    Caution: Electrical: isolate/disconnect the battery before wiring, use a correctly rated fuse or breaker, and use a qualified electrician if not confident.
  11. 11. Commission and verify airflow and cooling Fill the cooler's water reservoir, switch on the fan, and measure airflow at the crates with an airflow meter, aiming for the report's recommended ~0.5 m/s linear velocity entering the crates (p.21). Confirm the chamber cools toward the published performance: a 500 kg load cooled by 10 C in under 6 h using under 200 W (p.54; product page). For ongoing operation, keep the reservoir filled and clean the cooling pads roughly every 6 months (remove and hose off); pad life 2-3 yr, ~US$50 each; cooler 10-20 yr; panels 25+ yr (p.52).
    Caution: Keep hands/clothing clear of the fan; the chamber interior runs at high humidity, so check wiring and electrical connections are dry and sealed.

Known gaps

What the original design does not document:

  • No per-part price breakdown for the portable chamber is published upstream - only the unit total (US$1,100-2,300) and the cooler (~US$160) / pad (~US$50) line items. Every other item's unit_cost is left null.
  • Insulation-panel quantity and cut sizes are only in the appendix CAD diagrams/images (25-crate Nigeria design docs) and 3D renderings - not tabulated as a count in the text; the quantity=6 (box faces) is a derived estimate.
  • Bracket and hinge counts are not specified (report lists the items, not quantities); quantities above are engineering estimates.
  • No published water-consumption figure (L/day at operating conditions) for the portable or container chamber - water is a required but unquantified operating input.
  • Off-grid solar system per-component size/price for the portable chamber is not itemized (only the container pilot's US$5,600 oversized power and the US$900 India on-grid->off-grid delta).
  • 20-ft container FAECC: the full itemized per-line BOM (container, fans, wet pad, insulation, PV/batteries, labour) is the Section 6 'Construction Guidelines' PDF behind a free email-capture form at cooling-chamber.mit.edu - not retrievable without one email step; only two Kenya line items are in the public 2024 technical report.
  • Container CAD is a verified direct public download (125 MB, 84 SolidWorks/STEP files), but container BOM and priced build order remain gated.
  • Household clay-pot cooler BOM is fully local (inner clay/plastic/metal pot + outer clay pot/basin + 3-5 cm wet sand + burlap/cloth cover, per the 8-language assembly guide) with no purchased item prices; a local potter makes/repairs it.
  • No independent replication or peer-reviewed publication of the portable-chamber performance/cost; figures trace to CoolVeg and partners (ColdHubs, Artisana, Solar Freeze, Hunnarshala).
  • Mobile (transport) chamber is not cost-optimized (US$13,000) and lacks a widely available vehicle base - documented but not a buildable one-unit target.
  • No explicit numbered step-by-step is published for the portable chamber: the CoolVeg report gives the BOM (p.23), the insulation-panel cut-list (p.65), the airflow-pathway/geometry specs (p.21-22), and the build time + skills (p.26), but the ordered 11-step sequence above is reconstructed from those cited pieces — it is not a published instruction set.
  • The only published 'recommended order of construction' is for the container-based chamber (chamber site Section 6); its BOM + step-by-step document is a free email-gated download that could not be retrieved, so the container build could not be reproduced from retrieved sources.
  • Fastener quantities (number/size of brackets, screws, bolts) and exact panel-junction geometry are not in the retrieved text; the portable chamber's dimensional diagrams and 3D renderings are image pages in the report appendix and its CAD files are distributed as a zip on the chamber website — neither readable as build steps from text.
  • Off-grid solar + battery system sizing (panel wattage, battery Ah) for a specific chamber is not detailed; the report only states the chamber can be powered by off-grid solar and lists component lifetimes.
  • Ongoing water demand for continuous operation (litres/day) is not published; the report only states continuous cooling requires the cooling media to remain consistently wet.
  • The portable chamber's electrical wiring diagram / connection detail (cooler and optional controls to grid or solar) is not in the retrieved report text (the container documentation set has a Section 4 Electrical System Guidelines, also a gated download).
  • The mobile (trailer-based) chamber is described only qualitatively — same materials plus trailer/vehicle bed and a protective metal shell (p.24) — with no step-by-step; its build cost (US$13,000 off-grid, p.51) is not itemized.
  • The fully-local, no-power alternative unit — the clay-pot cooler (the project's scaled dissemination product) — has a complete published ordered assembly in the English guide (sieve/winnow sand to 3-5 mm; even sand base; center the inner pot with its top >=2 cm above the outer; pack the sides; water until it takes a few seconds to absorb, draining if the pot floats; cover with wet burlap/cloth; water ~1x/day; shade; no meat/dairy/medicines) but it is a different passive pot-in-pot device, not the flagship chamber documented in the steps above.

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • No measured water-consumption figure (L/day at operating conditions) is published for the forced-air chamber. The report's only water-consumption statement is qualitative: shielding the wet pads from sunlight/ambient 'reduces water consumption' and allows water recycling, versus a charcoal wall. J-WAFS separately states the water needed to operate the chamber is 'only a small fraction of the water consumed in growing the food.'
  • Project confirmed: CoolVeg is a real nonprofit founded 2022 by Eric Verploegen as an MIT D-Lab spinout; pilot forced-air evaporative cooling chambers (20-ft shipping container, 168 crates) are in operation in Kibwezi, Kenya (Solar Freeze, off-grid, $15,000) and near Bhuj, Gujarat, India (Hunnarshala Foundation, on-grid, $8,100), both with continuous interior/exterior temperature and humidity logging.
  • The $8,100 figure is the fully on-grid 20-ft container pilot built in 2022 in Bhuj, Gujarat, India with Hunnarshala Foundation: 168 crates, total cost including materials and labour, no solar system (reliable grid access); the report separately estimates an off-grid solar PV + battery adder of $4,000, giving a total of $12,100.
  • The $15,000 figure is the fully off-grid solar 20-ft container pilot built in 2022 near Kibwezi, Kenya with Solar Freeze: 168 crates, including materials, labour, and a 4 kW solar PV system with 16 kWh battery backup.
  • The plumbing is a closed recirculating loop: lower reservoir tank, submersible pump to an upper tank, electrically-actuated solenoid valves feeding the corrugated cellulose wet pads, and a collection tray that returns water to the lower tank. Therefore make-up (consumed) water equals evaporation loss only, not the pump flow rate.
  • The documented fan/water-pump hardware problem in BOTH pilots was component sourcing, not an in-field mechanical failure: the team sought DC fans and water pumps to avoid an inverter, could not find suitable DC fans after several months of searching, and instead installed AC fans, an AC water pump, and a DC-to-AC inverter (Kenya, off-grid) or plain AC (India, on-grid).
  • The wet pads are corrugated cellulose. For the Kenya pilot the pads had to be air-freighted from India to Kenya at a cost of US$1,900 due to sourcing and scheduling challenges; the report notes the pads are 'more durable and require less maintenance than a charcoal wall' but gives no field replacement interval.
  • Pilot context for lifetime cost: the fully off-grid Kenya chamber (168 crates, 4 kW PV + 16 kWh battery) cost US$15,000; the on-grid India chamber (168 crates) cost US$8,100 with an estimated further US$4,000 to convert to off-grid. The off-grid power system was deliberately oversized for the pilot (US$5,600) and expected to shrink once power requirements are better understood.
