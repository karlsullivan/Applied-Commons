Faircap (open water filter)

This project is under research: Applied Commons has catalogued it but has not written a build pack for it.

Open-collaboration water-filter project starting from 3D-printable filter designs, now a 0.1 micron membrane filter removing ~99.99% of bacteria (e.g. E. coli) for household jerrycans; open collaboration model, funded/supported by Elrha's UK-aid Humanitarian Innovation Fund, Oxfam, UK Aid, and MIT Solve. Targets low-income households without clean water and is designed to be reproducible locally. Lower maturity than Potters for Peace (design/production stage, Buy-1-Give-1 program).

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • IN-COUNTRY MEMBRANE SOURCING/REPLACEMENT: NO. Filters are made to order, manufactured in China, then shipped 2-6 weeks plus customs clearance. Per the ELRHA 2025 case study: 'Currently, no local manufacturers produce the filters or spare parts, so damaged filters cannot be easily replaced.' Documented consequence: in Palorinya (Uganda), households stopped using the filter after rodents damaged the spare parts and reverted to unsafe water. Faircap/ELRHA plan regional distributors or local manufacturers only 'if there is more demand.'
  • Limitations that bound the answer: the filter cannot remove salinity or dissolved organics, so it cannot treat high-salinity groundwater (the ACF Niger/Somalia contexts used low-salinity surface water - so those field results do not generalise to saline sources). It also does not remove taste/odour. Unit cost is about GBP 5 per humanitarian filter (~US$1 retail for the Mini).
  • WHO does not publish a single packaged 'filter + chlorine' household protocol, but explicitly endorses combining a filter with chemical disinfection as a multi-barrier strategy: the 2011 HWT performance-spec document lists as combined/multi-barrier configurations 'media filtration followed by chemical disinfection, media filtration followed by membrane filtration or composite filtration combined with chemical disinfection'.
  • Free residual chlorine (FRC) at the point of consumer delivery/collection: WHO chlorination guide (2017) requires a minimum 0.2 mg/L FRC maintained to the point of consumer delivery (and not exceeding 5 mg/L, aesthetic/health limit); WHO TN-11 (2013) specifies 0.2-0.5 mg/L at the point where the consumer collects water for normal domestic use; Sphere Standard 1 requires 0.2-0.5 mg/L free residual (raised to 0.5 mg/L in epidemic situations) at the point of delivery/consumption. The two are consistent: dose to a 0.2-0.5 mg/L residual at the household's collection/storage point.
  • NO field-validated membrane service life (litres + months) under high-turbidity tropical use is publicly published for Faircap. All public figures are maker/procurement specs: MSF Unicat (0.01um household) = 5,000 L total, ~1-year life span, 5-10 L/h, caveated to water quality and daily use; maker spec = ~10,000 L / ~2 yr (0.1um bacteria) and ~5,000 L / ~1 yr (0.01um virus). These are design claims, not measured field lifespans.
  • Membrane: high-efficiency 0.1um / 0.01um pore membrane (polysulfone per maker spec), gravity-fed, no chemicals/energy; removes turbidity, bacteria and protozoa, and ~99% viruses at 0.01um. Self-cleaning via continuous backwashing is designed to extend membrane life; early field tests found that disassembling to clean caused recontamination, which drove the self-clean redesign. Independently tested per WHO household-water-filter guidelines by Bachem AG (Switzerland) and the U. Barcelona Virus Research Lab (~99.99% bacteria/E.coli removal, Mini filter).
  • Stored-drinking-water recontamination during transport and storage is a separate, larger problem that no visual filter indicator solves; a 25-country study of ~59,633 households found 78% do not treat their water and E. coli persists in stored water, so it is detectable only by microbiological (E. coli) testing.
  • WHO's household water treatment documents (2011 performance spec; 2017 household guidelines, by title/known scope) set microbiological targets only and state they do NOT address dissolved chemical contaminants (arsenic, fluoride, nitrates, heavy metals, pesticides, disinfection by-products). So a membrane/porous filter alone does NOT guarantee chemical safety, which is exactly the gap the chlorine step does NOT close either (chlorine does not remove dissolved inorganics). Neither leg alone covers chemicals; the combined protocol covers microorganisms well but requires a separate argument for the chemical leg.
