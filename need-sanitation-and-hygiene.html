The need

Globally (2022 data, JMP/WHO): 1.5 billion people lack basic sanitation such as a private toilet or latrine; of these, 419 million defecate in the open (fields, bushes, water bodies); 57% of the world (4.6 billion) used safely managed sanitation and 88% (7.2 billion) at least basic. Where: 13 countries still have more than one in four people practising open defecation; since 2015 sanitation progress has been relatively flat in urban areas while the unserved increasingly concentrate in low-income countries; in dense, low-income urban areas sewerage is precarious, space for toilets is at a premium, and faecal sludge removal is unavailable or unaffordable. Affordability case-study countries (Ghana, Zambia, Pakistan, Uganda, Cambodia, Mexico) show basic WASH access unaffordable for a majority of households. Women and girls: 2.1 billion people aged 15-49 experience menstruation; children under 5 are the highest-risk group (in low-income countries children under 3 average 3 diarrhoea episodes per year; in conflict zones children are ~20x more likely to die from diarrhoea than from the violence itself).

1.4 million people die each year from inadequate drinking-water, sanitation and hygiene, the vast majority in low- and middle-income countries; unsafe sanitation alone accounts for 564,000 of these deaths, largely from diarrhoeal disease. Diarrhoea kills ~443,832 children under 5 and 50,851 children aged 5-9 every year (~1.7 billion childhood diarrhoea cases annually; third leading cause of death in children 1-59 months). Cholera: 1.3-4.0 million cases and 21,000-143,000 deaths worldwide per year. Poor sanitation also drives intestinal worm infections, schistosomiasis, trachoma, typhoid and polio, and exacerbates stunting/malnutrition. Persistence: despite 2.8 billion people gaining safely managed sanitation between 2000 and 2024, the 2030 targets are 'increasingly out of reach'; reaching universal safely managed sanitation would require a 5x increase in the current rate of progress, and climate change (floods, drought, sea level rise) is setting progress back.

What people do now

419 million people defecate in the open (down from 1.3 billion in 2000); 33% of the world (2.7 billion) use private facilities connected to sewers and 21% (1.7 billion) use toilets/latrines where excreta are safely disposed of in situ; the rest use basic, limited or unimproved facilities. 44% of household wastewater is discharged without safe treatment, and at least 10% of the world's population eats food irrigated by wastewater, much of it used unsafely. Handwashing: households are classified basic only if a handwashing facility with soap and water exists on-premises; facilities lacking soap or water, or using ash, soil or sand, count as limited. Costs of current practice (UNICEF/WHO 2021 affordability study): household WASH expenditure averaged 3.0% of total expenditure in Ghana, 4.1% in Pakistan, 2.4% in Uganda, 2.1% in Zambia, 1.5% in Mexico; in Ghana urban households spent 5.5% versus 1.7% rural, and including time costs the lowest-expenditure decile in Ghana spent 8.0%. For basic WASH service levels, a majority of households in Ghana, Zambia and Pakistan would pay more than 5% of total expenditure. Limited access to credit or ability to save prevents construction of quality household latrines, and growing water scarcity adds to access costs.

Where existing solutions fall short

Urban: sanitation progress has been flat in urban areas since 2015 while dense low-income/informal urban areas combine precarious sewerage, premium toilet space, and absent or unaffordable faecal sludge services. Safe management: only 57% of the world (2022) uses safely managed services, and large data gaps remain in monitoring whether on-site facilities are actually safely managed (containment, emptying, transport, treatment). Wastewater: 44% of household wastewater is discharged without safe treatment and at least 10% of the world's population consumes food irrigated by wastewater, much of it used unsafely. Hygiene: basic hygiene requires a 3x acceleration of current progress, and a large share of households has no handwashing facility or only a limited one (no soap/water). Affordability: basic WASH exceeds 5% of household expenditure for the majority of households in Ghana, Zambia and Pakistan, and affordability is not yet routinely monitored against SDG 6.1/6.2. Open defecation: remains above 25% in 13 countries. The WHO 2012 estimate (US$5.50 return per US$1 invested) indicates the scale of unmet, high-return demand.

What a solution must do

  • Contain excreta with zero discharge of wastewater or faecal sludge to the surface environment (JMP 'safely managed' definition, SDG 6.2.1a)
  • Ensure excreta are treated and disposed of in situ, or can be safely transported and treated off-site (JMP SMOSS service chain)
  • Facility not shared with other households (JMP criterion for at least basic sanitation)
  • Reduce faecal-oral pathogen transmission, measurable as reduced faecal indicator bacteria (e.g., E. coli) in water/soil downstream of the facility, since infection spreads through faeces-contaminated water and poor hygiene
  • Keep all-in household cost (materials, construction, operation, emptying) under 3% of total household expenditure (within the 3-5% affordability threshold used by multilateral development banks and the OECD)
  • Provide an on-premises handwashing facility with both soap and water, meeting the JMP basic hygiene ladder (ash/soil/sand does not qualify)
  • Provide a private place for menstrual material management for women and girls (JMP menstrual health indicator)
  • Function without reliable services for emptying, transport or treatment: must remain safe in settings where faecal sludge services are unavailable or unaffordable
  • Resist climate shocks: remain safe and functional through floods, drought and sea level rise (WHO identifies these as undermining existing services)

Constraints

  • Capital: limited access to credit or ability to save hinders construction of quality household latrines (UNICEF/WHO 2021)
  • Affordability ceiling: WASH spending above ~3-5% of household expenditure is considered unaffordable; in Ghana, Zambia and Pakistan basic WASH already exceeds 5% for a majority of households
  • Water: a growing share of the unserved population lives in water-scarce areas, raising the cost of water access for washing and flushing
  • Climate: floods, water scarcity, droughts and sea level rise set progress back and threaten existing services
  • Space: in dense, low-income urban areas space for toilets is at a premium
  • Service chain: faecal sludge emptying, transport and treatment services are unavailable or unaffordable in many urban and peri-urban settings; poorly designed/managed pits and septic tanks contaminate open drains and groundwater
  • Skills/literacy: solutions must be constructible and maintainable by local users, since the unserved population is concentrated in low-income countries where specialist service delivery is thin
  • Connectivity/power: most of the unserved population lives in low-income settings; grid power and connectivity cannot be assumed for operation or monitoring
