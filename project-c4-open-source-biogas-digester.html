CERN Open Source Hardware License v1.2 (registered with OSHW Foundation) biogas-digester drawings plus an Excel sizing spreadsheet that computes digester dimensions from your inputs. Processes organic/human waste anaerobically into biogas (cooking fuel) plus fertilizer. Open, documented, self-sized, locally buildable; early-stage but complete.

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • The model assumes 0.023-0.04 m3 gas per kg wet cow dung (average 0.0315 = (0.023+0.04)/2); with 40 kg/day feed that is 1.26 m3/day average (1.6 m3/day max). Household cooking need is computed at 1.26 m3/day (one 2-in stove, 0.33 m3/h, 2 uses/day), so the design has zero margin between production and demand.
  • No measured daily gas yield or measured methane content for the specific expanding-bag digester is publicly available; the project's own repository contains design drawings and the sizing spreadsheet only, with no measured field data. Field trials of flexible/bag digesters at household scale (MECS-TRIID Arusha trial; a 24-month 10 m3 tubular case) confirm cooking-energy adequacy but publish no m3/day figure.
  • Ethiopia, 2026 field study (Habte, Dananto & Nigatu, Energy Sustainability and Society, n=243 plants; national programme had installed 46,160 household systems by 2023): 27.1% fully functional, 19.8% partially operational, 53.1% not operational. This is a mixed-age snapshot, not a survival curve by plant age.
  • Nepal, 2025 study (Lohani et al., Scientific Reports, n=2,559 households across 10 districts): 54% of surveyed biogas plants non-functional or abandoned (~1,380 plants; ~Rs80,000 per plant, i.e. >Rs110 million wasted infrastructure in the sample); one district at 59% non-functional; even operational plants produced on average only 0.4 m3/day, less than half design capacity.
  • Failure mode 3 - maintenance and post-installation support absence: 68.3% of Ethiopian users received no post-construction support and only 27.2% received any post-construction training; the 2026 review attributes the 30-40% 3-5-year SSA failure rate to irregular feedstock supply, limited technical skills and poor access to spare parts; over 60% of rural digesters in SSA reported underperforming or abandoned for lack of maintenance, spare parts and user ownership.
  • No public source was found that measures helminth-egg or coliform pathogen reduction at exactly 95-day retention in a human-waste biogas digester. The 95-day retention is a design value in this project's own spreadsheet, and the project repo contains no measured pathogen data; the claim therefore rests on retention-by-design plus the supporting benchmarks below, not on a measured 95-day kill.
  • The model's yield range is consistent with, not above, the literature: animal manure yields ~20-30 m3 per tonne (0.02-0.03 m3/kg wet) per general references, and the model's 0.04 m3/kg ceiling equals the figure the project's own course publishes for cow dung at 30-40 C with 40-day retention. The 0.0315 average is ~5% above the top of the 0.02-0.03 band, so it is a modest, defensible (not optimistic) assumption.
  • Production drops below the household's cooking need in the cold/winter season. Because the design has ZERO margin (both production and demand are 1.26 m3/day), any temperature-driven drop in gas output breaches the need. Fermentation's optimum is 36 C and the technology 'especially applies for those living in a (sub) tropical climate', i.e. output falls meaningfully below the optimum - which in a non-tropical climate is the cold/winter months. The project's own sizing sheet independently notes retention time depends on temperature (40 days in the tropics to 50 days in cold climates), consistent with reduced/lengthened digestion in the cold. NOTE: the specific 25 C / '25.25 C' threshold that an earlier draft attributed to the project's training manual is NOT in any source this session could extract (the 85-page manual is image-based and its text layer yields no temperature sentence), so no such figure is claimed here.
