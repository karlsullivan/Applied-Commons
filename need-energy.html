The need

Approx 655M people without electricity in 2024 (UN custodian agencies - World Bank, WHO, IEA, IRENA, UN DESA - Tracking SDG7 2026; the IEA's own data puts the figure at 730M for 2024, a definitional difference). ~2.0B (WHO 2025: 2.1B) cook with polluting fuels. Sub-Saharan Africa: over 560M without electricity, 970M without clean cooking; five countries (Pakistan, Afghanistan, Mongolia, Myanmar, DPRK) hold 83% of developing Asia's remaining gap. ~1 billion people in low- and lower-middle-income countries are served by health-care facilities without reliable electricity (in sub-Saharan Africa 15% of facilities have no electricity at all). 2.1B people lack safely managed drinking water. 470M smallholder farmers lose up to 15% of income to post-harvest loss, a major driver of which is missing refrigeration. Rural is worst affected: 49% of the rural population cooks on polluting fuels vs 14% urban, and rural safely managed water coverage is only 60%.

Severe and persistent. Household air pollution causes ~2.9M deaths per year (2021) including 309,000 deaths under 5, and ~95M DALYs; it accounts for almost half of all pneumonia deaths in children under 5 and drives ischaemic heart disease, stroke, COPD and lung cancer in adults, with women and children bearing the greatest burden. Progress is stalling: only 11M fewer people lacked electricity in 2024 (the slowest pace since pre-pandemic), the pace of electrification must triple to reach universal access by 2030, and without action ~1.8B people will still rely on polluting cooking fuels in 2030 while sub-Saharan Africa's without-access count is projected to reach ~1B by 2027. In health care, only 40% of sub-Saharan African facilities (half of hospitals) have reliable electricity, undermining safe childbirth, vaccination and emergency care. The gap is structural: population growth in sub-Saharan Africa outpaces electrification, and remote areas may take 15+ years to close at the current pace.

What people do now

Lighting: kerosene lamps - rural households pay a median 35% more per litre than urban pump prices (Ghana 170% of the urban price, Kenya +46%), forcing a choice between other necessities and hours of light. Cooking: open fires and inefficient stoves on wood, dung, charcoal, coal or kerosene, producing indoor fine-particle levels up to 100x acceptable; clean-cooking access is 89% urban vs 56% rural. Pumping/irrigation: diesel, where fuel is 70-90% of the pumping system's life-cycle cost (diesel prices US$0.09-0.57/L across sub-Saharan Africa). Existing off-grid solar users typically get under 8 hours of electricity per day. Health facilities run diesel generators that are often not operational. Water: in many sub-Saharan African and Central/South Asian areas, women and girls spend 30+ minutes per day collecting water. A US$20 solar lamp already pays off in ~8 months of rural kerosene spending - the barrier is upfront cost and access to the product, not lifetime cost.

Where existing solutions fall short

Off-grid systems already serving ~86M people (11.1 GW of capacity, over three-quarters of beneficiaries in Africa) mostly provide under 8 h/day for basic lighting - too little for livelihood or health uses. The electrification pace (~11M people/year) is far below the tripled rate needed for 2030, and even at current rates only ~78% would have clean cooking by 2030 (~1.8B still short). Cold chain, mini-grids and health-facility electrification are constrained less by technology than by upfront capital, financing and business models, and O&M capacity (US$4.9B is needed just for minimal health-facility electrification in 63 LMICs). Kerosene and diesel remain the default because they are instantly available and up-front cheap - an open, locally buildable solution must beat them on lifetime cost and local maintainability, not just on health and emissions.

What a solution must do

  • Cooking (serves health): stoves and fuels that meet the WHO Guidelines for Indoor Air Quality - Household Fuel Combustion (PM2.5 and CO emission targets, ISO 19867 performance tiers) so indoor smoke drops from up to 100x acceptable levels to levels that remove the 2.9M deaths/year household-air-pollution burden
  • Health-facility power (serves health): reliable 24/7 electricity at a primary facility sufficient for vaccine and medicine refrigeration, medical devices, lighting, communications and clean water - the minimal/intermediate electrification levels the World Bank prices at US$4.9B across 63 low- and middle-income countries
  • Water (serves water/sanitation): power a solar borehole or pump delivering water to the household premises and available when needed (the JMP 'safely managed' bar), for a rural community or school
  • Food and income (serves food): power dry-season groundwater irrigation for a single-borehole smallholder plot (up to ~2 ha); regional cost modelling finds solar is cost-effective at installed cost <= US$2.5/Wp across much of southern and central Africa, with zero fuel cost vs 70-90% of diesel life-cycle cost
  • Food preservation (serves food): cooling capacity for a smallholder harvest or market node (cold room/chiller) so perishables survive farm-to-market time; lack of effective refrigeration contributes to the 14% of global food lost before reaching consumers
  • Lighting and safety (serves shelter/safety): at least 4 h/day of basic lighting plus phone charging (the international access definition), and aim for 8+ h/day - the majority of off-grid households today get under 8 h/day
  • Affordability (serves all needs): lifetime delivered cost under ~US$0.20/kWh, i.e. cheaper over its life than the kerosene and diesel the same household already buys - benchmark: a US$20 solar lamp pays off in ~8 months against the rural kerosene bill

Constraints

  • Off-grid solar installed cost is high (US$5-10/Wp all-in for home systems per the IRENA cost survey; the battery alone often exceeds the array's hardware cost), so design must minimize storage where possible (e.g., direct-coupled pumping without battery)
  • Many households cannot afford connection fees and wiring even where infrastructure exists - pay-as-you-go or phased financing is required
  • Remote settlements may not be grid-reached for 15+ years at the current pace - the solution must stand alone without a grid
  • Demand peaks in the dry season (irrigation) - systems must be sized for the peak month, not the annual average
  • Local O&M capacity is limited and diesel maintenance needs skilled labour - components must be serviceable locally with common tools
  • Fossil fuel prices are volatile (diesel US$0.09-0.57/L across sub-Saharan Africa; rural kerosene up to 170% of urban pump price) - the economics must hold at both extremes
  • International public clean-energy finance is small and concentrated (US$24.6B total; 80% of flows to just 25 countries per the SDG7 reports) - the business case must stand on local revenues, not aid
