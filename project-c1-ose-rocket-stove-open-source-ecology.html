OSE Rocket Stove (Open Source Ecology)
NeedAir and breathing
StatusParked
LicenceNone found
Climatestropical, arid, temperate, cold
This project is parked: Applied Commons has catalogued it but has not written a build pack for it.

Open, publicly documented solid-fuel rocket stove: burns fuel more completely than an open fire, producing less smoke and less CO with less fuel. Built from brick, clay and local materials; part of a large body of OSE documentation including build logs and linked rocket-mass-heater designs. Directly targets the 'combustion source' gap for households that rely on solid fuel; works year-round and in cold climates as cooking + heating.

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • The only RCT using a natural-draft rocket-style cookstove (Kirby et al. 2019, PLOS Medicine 16(8):e1002812, cluster-RCT in Western Province Rwanda, ~15,000 households) is NULL on PM2.5: 48-h personal exposure 218 ug/m3 (intervention) vs 223 ug/m3 (control) for cooks (beta=-0.089, p=0.486, n.s.); children 224 vs 231 ug/m3 (beta=-0.228, p=0.127, n.s.). The paper states adult and child exposures 'remained unchanged and above WHO's Air Quality Guideline (10 ug/m3) and Interim Target 1 (35 ug/m3) despite increased outdoor cooking.' Note this is PERSONAL (worn-monitor) exposure, not kitchen-area air.
  • WHO Interim Target 1 for PM2.5 is 35 ug/m3 as an ANNUAL mean (24-h companion level 75 ug/m3); the WHO 'clean' cooking-device criterion is an annual average at or below 5 ug/m3 (AQG) or the 35 ug/m3 IT1 stepping stone, applied to indoor levels maintained in actual use.
  • No retrieved public source reports an ANNUAL kitchen (cooking-area) PM2.5 measurement for a vented rocket stove, so compliance with WHO IT1's annual level of 35 ug/m3 cannot be demonstrated from the literature.
  • Premise confirmed (Kumar 2021, Environ Pollut 290:117997, 11 studies): standard-combustion ICS WITH A CHIMNEY gave the greatest PM2.5 reduction of all stove types; overall weighted-mean difference = −0.73 mg/m³ (95% CI −1.33 to −0.13) = −730 µg/m³. This is a 24/48-h kitchen/cooking-AREA difference (baseline vs post), NOT a personal-breathing-zone value and NOT an absolute concentration, so it cannot by itself establish 'below IT1'. No masonry heat-sink is isolated (the benefit is credited to 'with a chimney' broadly) and fuel moisture was not a controlled variable.
  • OSE Rocket Stove material cost is sub-US$100 with wide margin: OSE design target '< US$15 materials, no welding' (mass production intent); Appropedia CCAT reference build documented at exactly USD$84.02; Nepal field stoves at 4000-5000 NPR (approx US$30-38, 2022 rates); US DIY range US$30-120.
  • The only study measuring actual kitchen (cooking-area) 24-h PM2.5 in this setting (Rosa et al. 2014, PLOS ONE 9(3):e91011, pilot RCT, 121 monitored households) used an EcoZoom Dura improved wood stove that the paper describes as 'based on the rocket concept' with an internal chimney (a rocket-concept design, but different from the OSE's external-vented-flue rocket stove): 24-h cooking-area median 0.267 mg/m3 (~267 ug/m3) intervention vs 0.509 mg/m3 (~509 ug/m3) control, a 48% median reduction (p=0.005), and the authors state both arms were 'well over even the initial interim 24-h WHO target for PM2.5 (75 ug/m3).'
  • The null 2019 RCT result is attributed to cooking migrating outdoors and to persistent traditional-stove use (most households did not report the rocket stove as their main one), not to a true lack of emission reduction.
  • No retrieved source reports a PERSONAL breathing-zone PM2.5 measurement for a masonry or rocket stove. Every absolute figure is a kitchen/cooking-area 24-h mean or a modeled concentration. The question's specific framing ('personal breathing-zone below IT1') therefore cannot be answered yes from available data; by direction, a cook's personal exposure near the source would be at least as high as the (already ~10× IT1) area mean.
