The need

Primary users are pregnant women and newborns in low-resource, rural and conflict-affected settings (sub-Saharan Africa: 70% of the ~260,000 maternal deaths in 2023 and 57% of under-5 deaths; southern Asia: 17% of maternal deaths, neonatal mortality 21/1000), children under 5 (149.2 million stunted, 45.4 million wasted; ~45% of under-5 deaths linked to undernutrition), adults living with NCDs (43 million NCD deaths in 2021, 18 million premature deaths under 70, 82% in LMICs), people with mental disorders (1.2 billion in 2023; 75% of affected people in many low-income countries have no access to treatment; only 38 countries report a national suicide-prevention strategy), people with significant disability (1.3 billion, ~16% of world population, growing with ageing and NCDs), and patients overall facing cost barriers (World Bank: half the global population without essential health services; 2 billion facing severe financial hardship from healthcare costs; 1.6 billion at risk of impoverishment from out-of-pocket spending of 10%+ of household budget in 2022).

Severe and persistent, with a large inequality gradient: maternal mortality ratio 346 per 100,000 live births in low-income countries vs 10 in high-income (lifetime risk 1 in 66 vs 1 in 7,933); 37 conflict/fragile countries hold 61% of global maternal deaths with MMR 504 in conflict settings; neonatal mortality rate 27/1000 in sub-Saharan Africa (11x the lowest region), 2.3 million newborns died in 2022 and 64 countries will miss the 2030 SDG neonatal target without action; ~75% of neonatal deaths occur in the first week and ~1 million within 24 hours; 43 million NCD deaths annually, 82% of premature NCD deaths in LMICs; 727,000 suicides per year, 73% in LMICs and 3rd leading cause of death among 15-29 year-olds; people with schizophrenia die ~9 years earlier than the general population; progress is stalling (newborn-mortality gains reduced significantly since 2010; stunting is not declining in Africa).

What people do now

Current practice is a mix of partial formal care and out-of-pocket coping: skilled attendance at birth reaches only 73% of births in low-income and 84% in lower-middle-income countries (vs ~99% in high/upper-middle-income; 58% globally in 1990 rising to 81% in 2019), so many births proceed unattended - severe postpartum bleeding kills a healthy woman within hours if unattended, and infection persists where hygiene and early recognition are absent. Mental health care is scarce: only ~40% of people with psychosis and ~one third of people with depression receive formal care, and most affected people in low-income countries get no treatment at all; stigma and taboo suppress help-seeking (only ~80 WHO Member States have good-quality vital registration, so under-reporting is widespread). Financial costs push households into poverty: out-of-pocket spending of 10%+ of household budget put 1.6 billion people at risk of impoverishment in 2022; medicines are the largest public health expenditure after personnel in many low-income countries (up to 66% of public health spending) and drug prices are the largest single barrier to access, making medication a major cause of household debt. People therefore cope with informal/traditional care, delayed facility attendance, forgoing treatment, or debt-financed out-of-pocket purchases of often unregulated medicines (substandard/falsified medicines are growing in resource-limited settings).

Where existing solutions fall short

Existing solutions fall short in five places. (1) Coverage gap in skilled attendance: only 73% of births in low-income and 84% in lower-middle-income countries are attended by skilled personnel (vs ~99% in high/upper-middle-income countries), and most maternal deaths occur in low- and lower-middle-income countries where the known interventions (oxytocics, magnesium sulfate, skilled care) simply are not present at the moment of need - a last-mile delivery gap. (2) Newborn quality-of-care gap: 64 countries will miss the 2030 neonatal SDG target; 75% of neonatal deaths occur in the first week and ~1 million within 24 hours, precisely the window where current facility-centric care cannot reach home births; midwife-led continuity of care reduces baby deaths by 16% but is scarce. (3) Mental-health treatment gap: ~75% of affected people in many low-income countries receive no treatment (only ~40% of psychosis and ~1/3 of depression cases get formal care); only 38 countries have a national suicide-prevention strategy and only ~80 have good vital registration to even measure suicide - so the data, workforce, and financing foundations for mental-health service do not exist in most of the countries with the need. (4) Financial-protection gap: even where services exist, 1.6 billion people face impoverishing out-of-pocket costs (10%+ of household budget) and 2 billion face severe financial hardship from healthcare - WHO's 2025 goal is for 1 billion more people to benefit from UHC, and scaling PHC to deliver it requires an additional US$200-328B/year that low-income budgets cannot absorb without external support. (5) Workforce and resilience gap: the 11-million-worker shortfall, rural deployment failure, and international brain drain mean that even well-designed programs have no one to deliver them; and conflict/fragile settings (61% of maternal deaths) are structurally excluded from most programmatic approaches that assume stable institutions.

What a solution must do

  • Deliverable by non-specialist providers: core functions must be usable by community health workers, midwives, or trained lay providers after a training of no more than 2 weeks (task-sharing model, mirroring mhGAP), not requiring a physician, nurse specialist, or laboratory on site.
  • Cost ceiling: per-household or per-campaign unit cost of materials/consumables under US$50, with recurring consumable cost per user per month under US$2 where feasible, so it can be funded by out-of-pocket payments at low-income levels without pushing households past the 10%-of-budget impoverishment line.
  • Reach the unattended 27%: must operate in settings where skilled attendance is absent (rural, remote, conflict/fragile settings) - i.e., work from the last health facility or the home, with transportable, battery/solar-operable hardware (no reliable grid power, no clean water assumed).
  • Offline-first: must function with no internet connectivity (local data storage and batch sync when available), because target regions lack reliable connectivity; data must be usable with low-literacy interfaces (pictograms, voice, local language support for at least the dominant local language of the deployment area).
  • Early-detection sensitivity/specificity on measurable endpoints: for maternal risk, detect at least 4 of the 5 major complication clusters (postpartum bleeding risk, infection signs, pre-eclampsia/hypertension, obstructed delivery, unsafe-abortion history) at the community level with documented follow-up protocol; for newborns, support identification of the 4 leading causes (prematurity, asphyxia, infection, congenital anomaly) within the first 24 hours - the window in which ~1 million neonates die - with a documented referral or intervention action for each flag.
  • Mental-health coverage: include a validated screening + brief-intervention pathway (mhGAP-aligned) for the priority conditions (depression, psychosis, self-harm/suicide risk, epilepsy, alcohol use) that a non-specialist can complete in under 30 minutes per visit, with measurable outcomes: proportion of positive screens receiving at least one follow-up contact, and reduction in documented untreated major-depression prevalence in the served catchment.
  • Rehabilitation/ongoing support: support scheduled follow-up and adherence (missed-appointment rate below a defined threshold, e.g., <20% of scheduled contacts missed in 6 months) for NCD and disability conditions, since the 1.3 billion disabled and 43 million NCD-deaths population needs chronic, not episodic, contact.
  • Quality of evidence: outcomes must be evaluable against a control or pre/post baseline with at least 6 months of follow-up, reporting the specific numeric endpoints above - a solution that cannot produce these measurements does not qualify.
  • Maintenance: must be maintainable and repairable locally with parts available in the deployment country (no single global OEM dependency), with a documented spare-parts and repair procedure in local language; software must be open source with local-fork capability.

Constraints

  • Human resource shortage: projected global shortfall of 11 million health workers by 2030, concentrated in low- and lower-middle-income countries, with chronic under-deployment to rural/remote areas and international migration draining the lowest-income settings - solutions cannot assume specialist presence and must minimize training burden.
  • Power: target settings lack reliable grid electricity; equipment must run on batteries/solar and tolerate no-power operation for days to weeks.
  • Connectivity: intermittent or absent internet; no cloud-dependent workflows.
  • Water/sanitation and climate: deployments span tropical, arid, and conflict zones; hardware must tolerate heat, dust, humidity, and rough transport; hygiene-dependent functions (infection risk) must degrade gracefully where clean water is absent.
  • Materials and supply chains: local procurement constraints and the documented growth of substandard/falsified medicines mean any consumables must be spec'd with verifiable quality markers or produced locally from documented materials.
  • Literacy and language: low formal literacy and multilingual populations require pictogram/voice interfaces in local languages.
  • Budget: out-of-pocket affordability - per-user cost must stay below thresholds that push low-income households into the 10%-of-budget impoverishment line; national health systems in low-income countries spend up to 66% of public health budgets on medicines, leaving little fiscal space.
  • Security and access: 61% of maternal deaths occur in 37 conflict/fragile settings; solutions must be deployable where movement is restricted and institutions are fragile.
  • Regulatory: medical-device/drug regulation varies widely; WHO notes regulation lag delays availability - locally built solutions must navigate or operate in permitted categories (screening, education, task-sharing) where device registration is slow.
