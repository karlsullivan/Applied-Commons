The need

About 2.1 billion people (around a quarter of the global population) cook daily using open fires or inefficient stoves burning solid fuels (wood, animal dung, crop waste, coal) or kerosene, generating harmful household air pollution; most are poor and in low- and middle-income countries. In 2021, 49% of the global rural population relied on polluting fuels/technologies vs 14% in urban areas; in 2024 about 2.0 billion people primarily relied on polluting fuels for cooking, and sub-Saharan Africa alone had 923 million without clean cooking (2022). Separately, 99% of the world's population lives where ambient (outdoor) particulate-matter levels exceed WHO guideline limits, with 89% of ambient-pollution deaths in low- and middle-income countries and the heaviest burden in the WHO South-East Asia and Western Pacific regions. The burden falls disproportionately on women and children (who cook, gather fuel, and spend the most time near the hearth). Over 750 million people lack electricity, forcing reliance on polluting devices such as kerosene lamps that add fine-particle exposure.

Household air pollution caused an estimated 2.9 million premature deaths in 2021, including over 309,000 deaths of children under 5, and 95 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs); it nearly doubles the risk of childhood lower respiratory infection and is responsible for 44% of all pneumonia deaths in children under 5, 23% of adult COPD deaths in low- and middle-income countries, and 6% of lung-cancer deaths. Ambient (outdoor) air pollution caused an estimated 4.2 million premature deaths in 2019. The combined effect of ambient and household air pollution is associated with 6.6-6.7 million premature deaths annually, making it the second-highest risk factor for non-communicable disease. The harm is chronic and persistent: at current rates only 78% of the population will have clean cooking by 2030, leaving nearly 1.8 billion still exposed. It is also a leading cause of childhood poisoning (kerosene ingestion) and a source of burns and injuries, and fuelwood gathering exposes women and girls to musculoskeletal injury and, in insecure settings, violence.

What people do now

People default to the cheapest available heat: open fires and inefficient solid-fuel/kerosene stoves in poorly ventilated homes, where indoor fine-particle levels can reach about 100 times the acceptable level. This carries recurring costs: time spent cooking on inefficient devices and gathering/preparing fuel (which limits education and productive work and, for women and girls, adds injury and violence risk); safety risks from kerosene (burns, the leading cause of childhood poisoning, and carbon-monoxide exposure); and for the 750M+ without electricity, kerosene lamps that add fine particles at night. In high-pollution cities, current protection is largely reactive and individual: well-fitted respirators (e.g. N95) or grid-powered HEPA air purifiers used mainly during smog or bushfire episodes, which require reliable power and a continuing supply of filters and do nothing about the combustion source.

Where existing solutions fall short

Clean fuels (LPG, electricity, biogas) and WHO-certified efficient stoves are the proven health solution, but they underreach the poorest rural households: at current rates nearly 1.8 billion people still lack clean cooking by 2030. Commercial air purifiers and HEPA systems need reliable power many lack, are sold rather than locally buildable, and do not address the combustion source. N95/respirator masks address the symptom (inhalation) rather than the source, and depend on fit, tolerance over long cooking sessions, and a steady mask supply. Ventilation alone cannot reach the guideline when the pollutant is generated indoors by combustion. The unmet gap is a low-cost, off-grid, locally buildable intervention that reduces PM2.5 at and around the hearth (or in the breathing zone) without imported consumables or grid power.

What a solution must do

  • Cut indoor/breathing-zone fine particulate matter (PM2.5) exposure in a cooking/heating dwelling to at or below the WHO interim target 1 annual level (35 ug/m3) - which WHO estimates would avert roughly 300,000 deaths worldwide per year - with a design trajectory toward the 2021 Global AQG annual-mean PM2.5 guideline value of 5 ug/m3.
  • Cost: buildable from locally available materials at under ~US$100 of materials per unit, and maintainable by one person with common hand tools - cheap enough for the poorest rural household.
  • Operate reliably off-grid / on low power, since over 750 million people lack electricity and grid-dependent HEPA fans are out of reach for the target population.
  • Demonstrated effectiveness against fine particles/soot (PM2.5) at cooking-level exposures (not just gaseous pollutants), with a measurable before/after reduction.
  • Safety: introduce no new combustion byproducts, no kerosene or unprocessed coal, and add no carbon monoxide or fire risk to the home.
  • Climate robustness: functional in both hot-humid and dry-dusty conditions, with filters/components that are cheap to replace from local sources.
  • Verifiable: a simple, low-cost, portable PM2.5 sensor can be used to confirm the achieved reduction.

Constraints

  • Off-grid / unreliable or absent power in target rural low- and middle-income-country households - rules out high-power consumption.
  • Limited local manufacturing skill and tooling: must be buildable or assemblable with hand tools and common, locally available materials.
  • Hot, humid and dusty climates that degrade filters and electronics over time.
  • Little or no connectivity/telecom for data-logged or app-managed solutions.
  • Very tight household budgets and heavy labor already spent on fuelwood - low upfront cost and low per-use cost are mandatory.
  • Must not depend on a continuous imported supply chain of consumables (e.g. disposable filters) for daily operation.
