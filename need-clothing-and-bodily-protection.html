The need

826 million people in extreme poverty (under US$3.00/person/day, 2021 PPP; about 1 in 10 of the world; ~9% projected by 2030) who cannot afford adequate clothing/footwear; 3.6 billion in climate-susceptible areas (hardest hit in low-income countries and small island states, where extreme-weather death rates were 15x higher); 1.5 billion (24% of the world) infected with soil-transmitted helminths in poor-sanitation tropical/subtropical areas (sub-Saharan Africa, China, South America, Asia), mostly barefoot; outdoor workers exposed to UV (1.5M+ skin-cancer cases and 120k+ deaths in 2020); 830 million living with diabetes (2022) with vulnerable feet; ~200,000 new leprosy cases/yr in 120+ countries (skin and peripheral nerves); people in humanitarian/displacement settings lacking cold-weather gear and footwear.

Persistent and worsening. 37% of heat-related deaths are attributed to human-induced climate change; heat-related deaths among people over 65 up 70% in two decades; WHO projects ~250,000 additional deaths/yr by the 2030s from undernutrition, malaria, diarrhoea and heat stress alone; heat stroke (core >40 C) has a 21-65% fatality rate among hospitalized non-exertional cases (2003 and 2022 European heat waves killed >70,000 and 61,672 respectively). Cold: hypothermia below 35 C is life-threatening; frostbite is the freezing and destruction of living tissue, especially of the fingers, toes, hands, feet, nose, ears, cheeks and chin. UV: 15 million people are blind from cataracts, ~10% attributable to UV; ~1.2M non-melanoma and 325,000 melanoma skin-cancer cases in 2020. Drivers (extreme poverty at 1-in-10 and rising climate extremes) will not self-correct.

What people do now

Dominant practice is absence of protection: barefoot walking in poor-sanitation areas (the established transmission route for the 1.5-billion soil-transmitted-helminth burden); working outdoors without hats, covered clothing or UV-blocking sunglasses when the UV index exceeds 3; wearing no thermal clothing or insulated footwear in cold (producing frostbite and hypothermia); and for diabetes and leprosy patients, no therapeutic/protective footwear so ulcers and nerve damage progress to amputation and deformity. Where commercial goods exist, cost is the barrier: a person on under US$3.00/day (2021 PPP) spends the whole daily budget on all of life, so a pair of quality shoes, a UV garment or insulated winter clothing is a multiple-days-of-income purchase; most therefore rely on improvised, reused or donated clothing. Costs of this practice are medical treatment, lost labour, permanent disability, amputation, blindness, death, and the poverty trap itself as climate/exposure impacts push more people into poverty.

Where existing solutions fall short

Mass-market footwear and clothing are unaffordable for the 826 million in extreme poverty, who spend their entire daily income on all of life. There is no cheap, open, locally buildable design for UV-protective clothing or insulated cold-weather footwear for tropical/humanitarian settings (WHO's recommended UPF clothing + broad-brimmed hat + 99-100% UV sunglasses exists only as a product recommendation, not a local build spec). Diabetes and therapeutic foot-care footwear are expensive and essentially unavailable in low- and middle-income settings, so the 830 million with diabetes face amputation from untreated ulcers. Cold-weather gear for displaced/refugee populations is donation-dependent rather than locally produced, leaving seasonal gaps. Worker PPE that covers the body is itself a documented heat-stress risk factor, and no cheap locally made heat-protective garment fills that role. Barefoot walking remains the norm in soil-transmitted-helminth-endemic areas with no affordable durable locally made shoe acting as the parasite barrier. Onchocerciasis (river blindness) is a documented skin-and-eye parasitic disease of endemic river communities (blackfly bite as the route), where covering clothing would reduce skin contact - a protection angle inferred from the disease's skin involvement, not from a retrieved prevention recommendation. Note: ILO occupational-heat, UNHCR cold-weather and The Lancet authoritative heat-mortality pages were unreachable (403/404) from this environment, so no figures from them are cited; a global modelled heat-mortality total would strengthen the brief but is flagged unverified.

What a solution must do

  • Footwear with a puncture- and parasite-proof sole: blocks mechanical puncture (thorns, nails, glass) and penetration by soil-transmitted helminth larvae (hookworm/Ascaris/Trichuris); measurable via a standardized drop/penetration test with a steel thorn-analog probe and with infective larvae.
  • UV protection matching WHO's recommendation for UV index >=3: clothing with measured UPF >= 40 (50 preferred), a broad-brimmed hat covering eyes/face/ears/neck, and wraparound eyewear blocking 99-100% of UV-A and UV-B; measurable via a UPF (ISO 801) test and >=99% UVA/UVB attenuation.
  • Cold thermal insulation holding core temperature above 35 C for a defined exposure (e.g. continuous -10 C for >=4 h at low wind); measurable via a standard thermal-resistance (clo-value) test with a defined core-temperature margin.
  • Breathable, moisture-wicking construction for heat so sweat can evaporate (blocked evaporative cooling is the mechanism driving heat stress to heat stroke); measurable via moisture-vapour-transmission / wet-bulb-globe-temperature comfort testing at defined humidity.
  • Low cost: unit cost under ~US$20-50 for a footwear pair and under US$10 for a single garment (roughly a day to a week of income at US$3.00/day); measurable via bill-of-materials at local material prices.
  • Local buildability and durability: buildable without industrial machinery (hand sewing, simple molds, basic loom) from locally available materials (textiles, rubber/latex, plant fibres, local leather alternatives), durable across repeated wet-dry cycles, offered in child and adult sizes; measurable via a defined hand-tool-kit build and a wash/abrasion durability cycle count.

Constraints

  • Little or no electricity: no industrial sewing lines, no electric material heating.
  • Only hand tools and basic hand-sewing / loom / form skills assumed.
  • Must use locally available fibres, rubber/latex, plant materials and local leather alternatives, not imported specialty polymers.
  • Must serve the target climate: tropical heat and humidity, monsoon wetness, and in cold regions sustained sub-freezing temperature with wind.
  • Low connectivity; distribution must not depend on long commercial supply chains.
  • Unit cost must fit under US$3.00/day income levels and cover the full child-to-adult size range.
