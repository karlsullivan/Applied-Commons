The need

Everyone depends on environmental systems, but exposure and coping capacity are sharply uneven, so the burden lands on the poorest and most exposed. About 900 million people live in low-lying coastal zones and more than 1 billion are exposed to sea-level rise by 2050, with up to 1.2 billion potentially displaced. Small island developing States, river deltas, and low-lying coastal countries are existentially at risk (e.g. ~50 million people live below 5 m elevation in Bangladesh). Sub-Saharan Africa, South America and the Caribbean, South Asia, and southern/western Europe are where climate impacts hit food production hardest. The ~250,000 additional deaths per year (2030-2050) fall disproportionately on countries with weak health infrastructure, mostly developing countries. In short: billions of people, with the least-protected bearing the most.

Severe, persistent, and accelerating. Sea-level rise is already accelerating (the 2014-2023 rate is more than twice the 1993-2002 rate), with 15-30 cm of rise expected by 2050. Without this enabler, climate change adds ~250,000 deaths per year between 2030 and 2050 (malnutrition, malaria, diarrhoea, heat stress) plus US$2-4 billion per year in direct health damage by 2030; it will depress crop yields in most countries by 2050 (western-Africa millet yields already down 10-20% and sorghum 5-15% from warming); it pushes saltwater into freshwater sources, threatening drinking water, agriculture and health; and it could displace up to 1.2 billion people by 2050. Impacts compound over decades and some risk is irreversible (tipping points), so the window for cost-effective action is closing.

What people do now

People currently cope with reactive, project-based, externally funded adaptation and often-expensive grey works. International public adaptation finance reached only US$28B in 2022 (US$63B in 2021-2022 including more flows) against ~US$212B/yr of developing-country needs — a 10-18x gap. Where coastal communities protect, they frequently build hard grey defences (seawalls run roughly US$1,200 per linear foot in concrete, steel and labour plus ongoing maintenance), which cost more per protected metre than living shorelines and can raise damage on adjacent coast. Tree planting and reforestation proceed with little verification: only 18% of tree-planting organizations report monitoring and only 5% report survival rates, and sapling survival in some planting schemes is as low as ~52% at 18-20 months, so a large share of invested restoration is lost. Carbon removal currently delivers ~2 Gt CO2/yr via conventional land-use/forestry (novel methods add only 0.0013 Gt/yr) at roughly US$50-500 per tonne, against 7-9 Gt/yr needed by 2050. Net: coping is underfunded, expensive, and often fails to deliver durable carbon or protection.

Where existing solutions fall short

Existing solutions fall short in four places. (1) Carbon removal lacks both scale and durable, open MRV: only ~2 of the 7-9 Gt/yr needed by 2050 is delivered, novel methods are negligible, and there is no low-cost, open way to verify durable storage. (2) Ecosystem restoration fails silently: only 18% of tree-planting organizations monitor and 5% report survival rates, and sapling survival can drop to ~52% at 18-20 months without improved methods, so a large share of invested hectares is lost. (3) Adaptation finance and transformational implementation are far short: ~US$63B/yr of adaptation finance versus ~US$212B/yr of needs, with funding that remains reactive and project-based rather than anticipatory and strategic. (4) Grey coastal protection is expensive per metre and can raise adjacent costs, while the cheaper, durable, multi-benefit nature-based option is under-used because it demands local siting, long-term maintenance, and monitoring that communities and donors rarely provide.

What a solution must do

  • Food: restore at least 350 million hectares of degraded land by 2030 to preserve the soil, water and pollination services that food production depends on, while removing 13-26 Gt of greenhouse gases and protecting irrigation and groundwater recharge.
  • Water: provide nature-based coastal defence (mangroves, marshes, reefs) that measurably reduces storm-surge wave height and blocks saltwater intrusion into coastal aquifers, at a cost per protected metre below grey seawalls (~US$1,200/linear foot), verified across multiple field sites.
  • Shelter and safety: deliver durable protection for the ~900 million people in low-lying coastal zones that holds against 15-30 cm of sea-level rise by 2050 plus storm surges, with documented benefit-cost ratios of 2-10 and maintenance a community can operate.
  • Health: fund adaptation packages (heat, safe water, vector control, nutrition) that measurably cut the ~250,000 additional deaths per year projected for 2030-2050 and the US$2-4B/yr direct health damage, tracking reductions in heat, diarrhoeal, malaria and malnutrition mortality.
  • Food and climate: close the carbon-removal gap from ~2 Gt to 7-9 Gt CO2 removed per year by 2050, at no more than US$50-500 per tonne, with verified storage durability — decades-to-centuries for biological (vegetation/soil) and millennia for geologic — under open MRV.
  • Open and locally buildable: use planting and restoration protocols that achieve at least ~80% sapling survival at 18-20 months (versus lows of ~52%) and require 100% of projects to publish survival-rate monitoring (currently only 5% do), so outcomes are verifiable rather than assumed.

Constraints

  • Carbon removal at scale is not yet technologically available: society does not have the technology to implement CDR at Paris-target scale, novel methods are negligible (0.0013 Gt/yr), and reaching 7-9 Gt/yr by 2050 would need up to ~10 million workers and US$32B-US$1.1T of capex per gigaton per year.
  • Durability trade-off: biological CDR (vegetation, soils, sediments) stores carbon for decades-to-centuries and can release it back if the ecosystem degrades; only geologic storage (centuries-to-millennia) is durable, so biological solutions require long-term protection and MRV.
  • Restoration is capital- and labour-intensive and highly site-variable: mangrove restoration has a median cost of ~US$8,143 per hectare (range US$9 to over US$700,000), and success depends on planting method, native-species choice, and ongoing maintenance.
  • Finance: developing-country adaptation needs (~US$212B/yr by 2030) are 10-18x current international public adaptation finance (US$28B/yr in 2022), and in most arrangements the ultimate costs are borne by the developing countries themselves.
  • Site specificity: coastal protection value and blue-carbon value vary strongly by location (wave climate, elevation, salinity); one-size-fits-all designs fail, so solutions must be locally sited and locally maintainable.
  • Time horizon and irreversibility: sea-level rise is already accelerating and tipping points are a risk, so short-horizon or reversible measures are inadequate; solutions must be designed and funded on a decades-long horizon.
