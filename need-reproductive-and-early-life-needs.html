The need

Women of reproductive age and their newborns in low- and lower-middle-income countries, with the heaviest burden in sub-Saharan Africa and southern Asia; also conflict- and fragile-affected settings (37 countries = 61% of maternal deaths from only 25% of live births). Scale: ~260,000 maternal deaths/yr (92% in LIC/LMIC; SSA 182,000, S. Asia 43,000); ~27 million women affected by postpartum haemorrhage/yr (~1 in 6 births); 2.3 million newborns dying in the first 28 days in 2022 (~6,500/day; SSA NMR 27/1,000, C&S Asia 21/1,000); 13.4 million preterm births in 2020 (majority S. Asia + SSA); and ~224 million women with unmet need for safe, effective contraception (mainly developing regions).

Mostly preventable but fatal and persistent. A maternal death occurs roughly every 2 minutes (global MMR 197/100,000 live births in 2023 vs the <70-by-2030 target); a woman's lifetime risk of maternal death is 1 in 66 in low-income countries vs 1 in 7,933 in high-income. Postpartum haemorrhage alone causes ~45,000 maternal deaths/yr and leaves survivors with surgical need and lifelong reproductive disability. In low-income settings half of babies born <=32 weeks and >90% of extremely preterm (<28 weeks) babies die within days (vs <10% in high-income); survivors face lifelong disability. The four leading neonatal causes (prematurity, birth asphyxia/trauma, neonatal infection, congenital anomalies) still account for 4 in 10 under-5 deaths in 2022 as in 2000, and neonatal mortality gains have stalled since 2010 (64 countries will miss the 2030 SDG); conflict areas have MMR of 504/100,000 vs 99 in stable settings.

What people do now

Care is dominated by skilled attendance, but it is sharply unequal: ~99% of births in high- and upper-middle-income countries are assisted by a skilled provider vs only 73% (low-income) and 84% (lower-middle-income); poor, remote women are least likely to receive care. ~80% of births are facility-based, but few mothers/newborns stay the recommended 24-hour highest-risk window, so many newborns die at home from early discharge, access barriers and delayed care-seeking. The preventable causes are known and treatable with well-established care (oxytocics after birth for bleeding, antibiotics for infection, magnesium sulfate for pre-eclampsia, skilled delivery, safe abortion and post-abortion care), yet uptake is slow, diagnosis is late and commodities are inconsistent. The cost of the status quo: tens of thousands of PPH deaths (~43,000-70,000/yr) and >US$10 billion/yr in PPH costs to countries, systems and families; unmet-need contraception drives unintended pregnancy, unsafe abortion and preventable maternal death, while every US$1 spent closing that gap yields ~US$27 in economic benefits.

Where existing solutions fall short

Existing solutions fall short chiefly on access, implementation and measurement. Access: skilled attendance is 73-84% in LIC/LMIC vs 99% in HIC, and conflict/fragile settings face the highest risk because skilled attendance, PPH commodities and emergency obstetric care are often inaccessible. PPH implementation: proven interventions are adopted slowly - late diagnosis, inconsistent commodities, and an innovation landscape stagnant for ~30 years with fragmented guidance across global, regional and national levels. Newborn home deaths: many newborns die at home from early discharge, access barriers and delayed care-seeking despite ~80% facility births; few stay the recommended 24 h. Preterm care: in low-income settings half of <=32-weekers die from lack of simple, cost-effective care, and suboptimal technology in middle-income settings increases disability among survivors. Reproductive choice: ~224 million women still lack safe, effective family planning, driving unintended pregnancy, unsafe abortion and preventable maternal death. Measurement: 64 countries will miss the 2030 SDG neonatal target, and data gaps mean many newborns and stillbirths are never counted.

What a solution must do

  • Prevent/treat postpartum haemorrhage at the point of birth via a 'PPH bundle' with objective quantitative blood-loss assessment, targeting the ~1-in-6 incidence.
  • Provide a heat-stable uterotonic (e.g. carbetocin) that remains effective through ~37-40C transport and cold-chain gaps.
  • Provide a reusable, objective blood-loss measurement tool (pad/mattress weighing or sensor) accurate enough to trigger treatment early rather than by estimation.
  • Provide power-free neonatal thermal care (kangaroo mother care / immediate skin-to-skin) supporting 8-24 h/day skin-to-skin for preterm/LBW infants at facility or home, with no grid electricity.
  • Provide a manual, self-inflating, reusable resuscitator/bag-mask for newborns to ~10 kg, operable by a trained midwife or community health worker, for birth asphyxia.
  • Deliver a defined essential-newborn-care package at birth and in the first days: hygienic cord/skin care, early and exclusive breastfeeding, danger-sign assessment, and prophylaxis (BCG, hepatitis B, vitamin K, ocular prophylaxis).
  • Stretch: support preterm survival beyond warmth (infection and breathing-difficulty management, e.g. CPAP, caffeine) consistent with the finding that ~3/4 of preterm-birth deaths are preventable with current cost-effective interventions.
  • Close the reproductive-choice gap with effective modern-contraception delivery (including safe abortion/post-abortion care where legal) that measurably reduces unmet need among the ~224 million women.
  • Keep per-unit materials/production cost low and locally buildable (passive or low-tooling; well under ~US$100 per unit), and where electricity is required, assume battery/solar rather than mains.

Constraints

  • Power: off-grid / intermittent electricity - prefer passive or battery/solar devices over mains-dependent equipment.
  • Skills: usable by midwives, community health workers and (where appropriate) families after short training, not specialists alone.
  • Climate/medicine stability: heat-stable formulations and packaging that survive ~37-40C and high humidity without a reliable cold chain.
  • Materials/tooling: locally available, low-cost, reusable where practical; hygienic cord care and low-contamination practice assumed.
  • Connectivity: must operate fully offline; no cloud dependence for the point-of-care function.
  • Settings: must work in home, first-contact and facility settings, including fragile/conflict and remote areas where skilled attendance and commodities are scarce.
  • Scale/affordability: per-birth and per-unit cost must be affordable at scale in low-income countries (cost-effectiveness bar, not premium).
