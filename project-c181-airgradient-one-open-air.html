Open-hardware/open-source community air-quality monitors (indoor ONE, outdoor Open Air) measuring PM2.5, CO2, TVOC, NOx, temperature and humidity; fully open design (schematics, KiCad files, enclosures, firmware) with 30,000+ citizen deployments, and data exportable to any server (e.g. Home Assistant). Headquartered in Sweden (long freezing winters, low winter sun); battery-capable outdoor designs make it usable year-round in cold climates. Closes the neighborhood-resolution air exposure gap.

Assembly

Tools: Torx T6 screwdriver (included in kit), 5V 2A USB-C power adapter (NOT included; builder must supply; weather-rated if outdoor), Smartphone, tablet or laptop with Wi-Fi (for onboarding), Data-capable USB-C cable (included 90-degree cable works; charge-only cables do not), Paperclip/toothpick/small screwdriver (only if manually flashing firmware - to reach the BOOT button), Computer with a Chromium-based browser (Chrome/Edge/Chromium; only for manual firmware flashing via WebSerial), Soap or light oil (optional, to lubricate enclosure screws if the mini screwdriver lacks torque)
Skills: No electronics or soldering background required (official: 'anyone ... no soldering is required'), Basic hand-tool use (screwing, following pictured on-screen steps), Basic Wi-Fi configuration (joining a device hotspot, entering a network password), (Optional) Browser-based serial flashing only if manual firmware update is wanted
Time: Officially ~15 minutes from opening the box to a functioning monitor (assembly + first power-on); add a few minutes for the Wi-Fi onboarding. CO2 readings need a few days of auto baseline calibration before they are accurate; TVOC needs ~1-2 minutes to warm up.

  1. Unbox and inventory the kit Confirm you have: AirGradient ONE PCB v9 (ESP32-C3-MINI + OLED + pin sockets), Plantower PMS5003 PM sensor (pre-mounted on the board with cable), SenseAir S8/S88 CO2 module, SHT4x temperature/humidity module, SGP41 TVOC/NOx module, 90-degree USB-C cable, ASA plastic enclosure, two stands, and 4x M1.8x10 Torx screws. Check the PCB version number printed under the AirGradient logo so you follow the right guide.
    Caution: None. Verify the power adapter is a 5V 2A unit before proceeding - it is not included in the kit.
  2. Install the SHT4x temperature/humidity module Insert it into the slot at the bottom-left of the board labelled 'SHT1', with the AirGradient logo facing outward. Do not confuse it with the SGP41 TVOC module, which looks very similar.
    Caution: If the SHT4x and SGP41 modules are swapped, the device still works but the temperature/humidity and TVOC readings will be wrong.
  3. Install the SenseAir S8/S88 CO2 module Seat it in the pin sockets labelled 'CO2 Sensor' with the correct orientation so all pins slide in. S8 and S88 are functionally identical and mounted the same way.
    Caution: By default the CO2 sensor runs a 7-day automatic baseline calibration, so it needs a few days in a real environment to become accurate; a PM/CO2 value of -1 or -3 on display means the module is not fully connected.
  4. Install the SGP41 TVOC/NOx module Mount it in the top-left corner slot labelled 'I2C3', oriented so the sensor faces outward. Re-check that the SHT4x and SGP41 are in their correct, logo-outward positions.
    Caution: The TVOC module shows 0 until it has warmed up for ~1-2 minutes; NOx commonly reads 0 or 1 and only rises at high concentrations.
  5. Record the PM sensor serial number Look at the blue Plantower PMS5003 from the bottom of the board and note or photograph the first eight digits of the serial number on its sticker. These are needed to apply AirGradient's PM batch corrections for accuracy.
    Caution: Write it down now to avoid having to reopen the monitor later.
  6. Power-test the bare PCB Plug the 5V 2A USB-C adapter into the board. Within a few seconds the OLED display should light up.
    Caution: Use a standard 5V 2A supply; a lower-rated adapter can cause a distorted screen or sensors dropping out. If the device does not turn on or restarts randomly, try a different USB adapter.
  7. Connect to Wi-Fi (onboarding) On power-up the device broadcasts a hotspot named airgradient-<serial>; join it from a phone/laptop (hotspot password: cleanair). The Wi-Fi Manager opens automatically at 192.168.4.1; tap Configure WiFi, select your network, enter the network password (not 'cleanair'), and leave 'Prevent connection to AirGradient servers' unchecked if you want the cloud dashboard and automatic firmware updates. The network must be 2.4 GHz only, with internet, and no WPA Enterprise, captive portal, or firewall blocking unknown MACs.
    Caution: iOS may warn 'No Internet Connection' on the hotspot - this is expected; temporarily disable Cellular Data and keep connected to the hotspot until setup completes. If the password is wrong you are returned to the network list; if the portal times out the device reboots.
  8. Fit the PCB into the enclosure and close it Place the PCB assembly inside the enclosure - the PCB needs no screws; the enclosure holds it firmly. Close the enclosure with the four long M1.8x10 screws, inserted through the ventilation vents around the back.
    Caution: The included mini screwdriver may not have enough torque; use a larger T6 or put soap on the screws. Do not force them - forcing can strip/stick them in the case and make the device hard to open for maintenance.
  9. Mount / place the monitor Wall-mount via the M4 holes on the left and right (aligned to US/EU junction boxes) at 150-250 cm height, or stand it on a desk/shelf using the included plastic stands (slid into the bottom vents). Route the USB-C cable down the channel on the back; only the included 2 m 90-degree cable lets it sit flush against a wall.
    Caution: Keep it out of direct sunlight and off hot walls, at least 90 cm from any AC or ventilation outlet, and away from direct pollution/heat sources (ovens, fireplaces, large appliances) to avoid skewed readings.
  10. (Optional) Manual firmware flash via browser Only needed for a manual update - the device ships with firmware and auto-updates once on Wi-Fi. Press and hold the BOOT button, plug in the data-capable USB-C cable while holding it, then release; the device looks off (no screen/LED). Open the AirGradient firmware page in a Chromium browser, choose Flash Firmware, select the 'USB JTAG / Serial Debug' port, and click Install Firmware (optionally 'Erase all device data').
    Caution: WebSerial flashing only works in Chrome/Edge/Chromium (not Safari/Firefox) and a charge-only cable will not work. Do not disconnect the cable during flashing; close other serial apps if the port is not visible.

Known gaps

What the original design does not document:

  • No per-part supplier/pricing is published for the main board (bom.csv has an empty 'Supplier and ref' column) — TPL5010, LM1117, USB-C, passives, fuse, button, LEDs, PicoBlade and I2C socket costs must be sourced from a distributor (left null).
  • Upstream does not publish a single consolidated BOM for a finished O-1PST unit (parts are split across the main-board repo and per-module repos); the O-1PST configuration (which modules fit which slots) is inferred from the spec sheet + assembly instructions, not one explicit part list.
  • Enclosure cost/quantity is not documented (no BOM line); only the material (ASA) and dimensions are given. Whether the enclosure is injection-molded (assembled) vs printed (kit) per purchase variant is not stated in a BOM.
  • No cost or part number for the 4 m USB cable or the 5 V/2 A power adapter (explicitly not included) — both are required to run the unit but priced only as generic commodities.
  • No documented long-term local data storage/part for the standard Open Air — the firmware partitions reserve a 128 KB SPIFFS + 20 KB NVS on the 4 MB ESP32-C3 flash (partitions.csv); there is no separate storage part in the BOM, so backhaul disconnection means no buffering (a practical_independence dependency already flagged).
  • The Max solar/battery off-grid power design is not ported to the standard Open Air — no battery/BMS/solar-charger part appears in the O-1PST BOM, so the 'small off-grid solar/battery + local buffering' constraint in the need brief is NOT met by the standard unit's BOM (only the Open Air Max, a different product, addresses it).
  • Operating envelope for outdoor use: S8 CO2 channel rated 5-30 C / 20-70% RH (spec sheet) — no heated/enclosure-derating part or spec is provided to keep it in range year-round in cold/wet climates.
  • No STEP-format enclosure CAD published for the current models (ONE I-9PSL, Open Air O-1PST, Open Air Max) - the overview index offers STL archives only, although the O-1PST build page's introduction claims 'enclosure step files' are freely available.
  • No public bill of materials with part numbers, supplier links or per-part prices for self-sourcing the current ONE/O-1PST versions - components are documented in the kit build instructions but kits are sold as a bundle.
  • Schematics are only available inside the KiCad project archives; no standalone rendered schematic PDF is published for the current models.
  • No CAD/PCB design files at all for the 2026 AirGradient Go portable monitor - only its firmware exists (Open-Air-Foundation/firmware-core monorepo, products/go).
  • The Open Air Max is documented in the index as 'only available fully assembled' (N/A build instructions) even though its KiCad files and STLs are downloadable.
  • The 5V/2A USB-C power adapter for the AirGradient ONE kit is not included and no adapter model/spec is recommended in the build documentation.
  • No power adapter is included in the kit and the assembly page gives no specific adapter model or purchase link - only the 5V/2A spec - so sourcing a compatible supply is left to the builder.
  • No torque specification (in Nm) is documented for the enclosure screws; guidance is purely qualitative ('do not force', 'use soap or a larger T6').
  • The assembly pages are image-heavy; the step-by-step is documented here from the text descriptions, and the accompanying photos/video (e.g. 'as per the picture below') are not available as text, so exact visual orientation cues must come from the linked video/pictures.
  • No assembly-side instruction is given for actually applying the PM-sensor batch correction to the firmware - the guide only says to record the serial and points to a separate article.
  • The assembly guide does not document a fully offline/local-only build path (no cloud, no OTA); local HTTP/MQTT/Home Assistant are mentioned only as integrations, not as an assembly step, so a connectivity-free deployment is not covered in this section.
  • No DIY self-sourcing build procedure (sourcing individual components) is given in the assembly section; the overview only links to kit builds, so a parts-level BOM and where to buy each component is not documented here.
  • No IP ingress rating is published for the O-1PST (enclosure called 'weatherproof' but no IP code / rain-vs-dust quantification)
  • No published long-term field drift/reliability study for the outdoor O-1PST specifically (the 15,000-day / London 2-year 'no drift' results are vendor co-location; the peer-reviewed SAGE study is on the indoor 'Pro')
  • No lab-chamber (controlled T/RH/interferent) validation for the O-1PST - AQMD itself notes chamber testing 'may be necessary' to fully evaluate performance
  • No published MTBF / expected service life or field failure-rate for the deployed unit; only the PM (~2 yr) / CO2 (~10 yr) sensor-replacement schedule is given, and that is from the indoor ONE manual
  • No documented long-term local measurement storage on the standard model - data is lost during a WiFi outage (spec: 'Internal memory: No')
  • The Max solar/battery off-grid design is NOT ported to the standard Open Air
  • No formal, published acceptance-test protocol or recognized test standard (EPA/ISO) exists that a finished O-1PST can be graded against; the EPA targets come from the PMS5003/PurpleAir co-location work, not a standard for this device
  • No acoustic/vibration/shock or wind-load rating for the mounting hardware is published
  • No EMC/EMI detail beyond the CE/FCC/RoHS/REACH/FCC-ID marks
  • No component-level (sensor-board) BOM cost is documented in the retrieved sources; the ~$225 (AQMD price) and 'assembled ~$190 / kit ~$125' figures are not itemized (the one independent off-grid build itemizes only the ~$144 power add-on, not the monitor's own BOM)

Safety

Low-voltage (5 V SELV), battery-less, passive-sensor device with CE/RoHS/REACH + FCC ID 2AC7Z-ESPC3MINI. No significant hazard to people; safe with a handful of low-severity controls around the external power supply, weather protection of the USB connectors, correct mounting, and ESD/polarity during assembly. No qualified-person step is required.

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • The 4 MB flash has no partition dedicated to storing measurements. partitions.csv gives: nvs 20 KB, otadata 8 KB, app0 ~1.9 MB (0x1E0000), app1 ~1.9 MB (0x1E0000, OTA), spiffs 128 KB (0x20000), coredump 64 KB. The only general-purpose data partition (SPIFFS, 128 KB) is used only for the configuration file (AgConfigure.cpp opens CONFIG_FILE_NAME for read/write); nothing writes measurement data to flash.
  • WiFi mode performs no buffering. postUsingWifi() (OneOpenAir.ino) builds the current in-RAM measurement average via measurements.toString(...) and calls a single httpPostMeasures; on failure it only prints to serial ('Online mode and isPostToAirGradient = true') and does not queue, retry, or persist the data. When WiFi is down, networkingTask() hits 'wifiConnector.isConnected()==false' and does delay(1000); continue, so no transmission happens. A multi-day WiFi disconnect therefore produces a data gap with no backfill on reconnect.
  • Native reporting cadence is not 5 minutes: WIFI_MEASUREMENT_INTERVAL / WIFI_TRANSMISSION_INTERVAL are 1*60000 ms (1 min) and CELLULAR_MEASUREMENT_INTERVAL / CELLULAR_TRANSMISSION_INTERVAL are 3*60000 ms (3 min). A 'days of 5-min backlog on 4 MB flash' framing does not match the implementation; the correct frame is 'no flash store' + 'WiFi = no buffer (0h)' + 'cellular = ~4h in-RAM buffer'.
  • There is no flash/SD/CSV/file-based measurement store or backlog anywhere in the firmware or the client library. A full grep of the main example (OneOpenAir.ino), the client, and the config layer for .csv/.log/.dat/SDCard/SD./createFile/history/ringBuffer/ring_buffer/FIFO/dataBuffer/persistence returns zero hits; NVS/Preferences are not used to store measurement values. So there is no 'N days at 5-min resolution' capacity to quote, because no such store exists.
  • Cellular mode DOES buffer, but only in RAM, not flash. A static AirgradientPayload measurementPayload holds payloadBuffer[] (array of MAXIMUM_PAYLOAD_BUFFER=100 in airgradientClient.h), and the firmware caps it at MAXIMUM_MEASUREMENT_CYCLE_QUEUE=80 (OneOpenAir.ino). newMeasurementCycle() appends one cycle per tick and, at capacity, drops the OLDEST entry (memmove). On a failed CoAP post it logs 'Post measures failed, retry in next schedule (data retained)' and keeps the buffer; on success it sets bufferCount=0. The cellular cadence is CELLULAR_MEASUREMENT_INTERVAL = 3 * 60000 ms, so 80 cycles x 3 min = ~240 min = ~4 hours of maximum backlog before the oldest samples are silently dropped.
  • The 'offline' setting is not a buffer. docs/local-server.md documents offlineMode as: 'the device will disable all network operation, and only show measurements on the display and ledbar' (Read-Only, changed via reset button on boot). AgConfigure.cpp isOfflineMode() = (offline || _offlineMode) and the state machine shows an 'offline mode' display state. It is a manual display-only mode, the inverse of a data-retention buffer.
  • No public source found reports a measured steady-state power draw of the standard O-1PST specifically at 5-minute sampling. The 1.9-2.8W figure is the closest available and is at the default 1-minute rate; draw at 5-minute sampling would be expected to be similar or slightly lower but is not documented.
  • The Open Air Max ships with a 10W (18V) solar panel and three 18650 batteries (~10,000 mAh, user-replaceable). A fully charged battery powers the device roughly 4-5 days (KB) to 5-6 days (manual v3) without sunlight, and it can operate on an average of about one hour of direct sunlight per day; a second panel is recommended for high-latitude, cloudy, or shaded sites.
