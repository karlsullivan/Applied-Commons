The need

Who lacks mobility and independent living, and where: 1.3 billion people (~16% of the world's population) live with significant disability, concentrated in settings with inaccessible transportation, public buildings and housing (WHO). 2.5+ billion people need one or more assistive product, projected to reach 3.5 billion by 2050 (WHO). 2.4 billion people live with a health condition that may benefit from rehabilitation; in some low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) more than half of them receive no rehabilitation services (WHO). Mobility-specific: of the 80 million people who need a wheelchair, only 5-35% have access to one depending on country (WHO/Global Report on Assistive Technology 2022, via WHO fact sheet). Hearing: 430 million people have disabling hearing loss, ~80% in LMICs, including 34 million children (WHO). Ageing: 1 billion people were 60+ in 2020 (12% of population), rising to 1.4 billion (1 in 6) by 2030 and 2.1 billion (22%) by 2050, with 80% of the world's older people in LMICs by 2050 (WHO). Households with persons with disabilities are disproportionately food insecure due to higher poverty and lower employment (World Bank).

Severe and persistent, not episodic. Falls: 684,000 deaths per year (second leading cause of unintentional injury death), 37.3 million falls severe enough to require medical attention, >38 million DALYs lost per year; >80% of fatal falls in LMICs; in the US 20-30% of older people who fall suffer moderate-to-severe injury (WHO). Wheelchair-dependent people in the developing world, before receiving even a simple donated chair: 46.6% never left home, 70.3% reported daily pain, 52.3% were hospitalized at least one day per month - i.e., housebound, in chronic pain and repeatedly hospitalized (Shore & Juillerat 2012, peer-reviewed). Pressure sores from poorly fitting seats force long-term wheelchair users into hospital care or immobility; cheap foam cushions fail within a year of use in durability tests (Guimaraes & Mann 2003). Unaddressed hearing loss costs the world almost US$1 trillion per year (WHO). Persons with disabilities die earlier, have poorer health and more limitations in everyday functioning than the general population (WHO). The need is structural and grows with population ageing: need is projected to grow from 2.5B to 3.5B people by 2050 while access stays stuck (WHO).

What people do now

What people do now and what it costs: (1) Most people in low-income countries get nothing - access to the assistive products they need is as low as 3% in some low-income countries vs ~90% in some high-income countries (WHO). (2) Many rely on erratic donations/charity: donated products are often of low quality or inappropriate for the person's needs, and users are not taught how to use them safely and get no follow-up (WHO). A simple donated 'depot-style' wheelchair is currently one of the few things that work: after 12 months, 519 recipients in 3 developing countries improved their independence by 11%, health and mood by ~20%, and pain fell significantly - but it only reaches a fraction of the 80 million who need chairs, and the baseline condition it replaces is housebound isolation with daily pain and monthly hospitalization (Shore & Juillerat 2012). (3) Those who can pay buy from pharmacies, clinics or workshops at out-of-pocket cost; even in high-income countries assistive products are often rationed or excluded from health/welfare schemes, forcing out-of-pocket payments (WHO). (4) The health system absorbs the consequences: average cost per fall injury for people 65+ is US$3,611 (Finland) and US$1,049 (Australia); wheelchair users with pressure-related complications face repeated hospitalization; households pay the care burden themselves, and PwD households are disproportionately food insecure (WHO; World Bank). (5) Community workaround that works but is ad hoc: e.g., a seat cushion built from bicycle inner tubes and plastic balls (the 'Tuball') performs pressure distribution better than the foam cushions actually used in Brazil and survived at least one year of fatigue testing where foam broke down - evidence that locally buildable, locally sourced solutions can outperform imported cheap ones (Guimaraes & Mann 2003).

Where existing solutions fall short

Where existing solutions fall short: (1) The access gap: 3% (some low-income countries) to 90% (some high-income countries) - even the WHO-recognized 'simple' donated wheelchair reaches only 5-35% of the 80 million who need one (WHO). (2) Quality of what does arrive: donated/charity products are frequently low quality or ill-fitting, with no user training and no follow-up or repair service (WHO). (3) Durability of cheap imported products: foam cushions used in the developing world break down within about a year of use in fatigue tests, while a bicycle-inner-tube design survived - i.e., the cheapest imported option is the least durable, and no cheap locally buildable seat solution is standard (Guimaraes & Mann 2003). (4) Workforce and service gap: >50% of people needing rehabilitation in some LMICs receive none; services are concentrated in cities with long waiting times; fewer than 10 skilled practitioners per 1M in many LMIC settings (WHO). (5) Systems gap: very few countries have a national assistive technology policy or programme; funding and procurement mechanisms are missing, so provision is left to the private sector and charity - even in high-income countries products are rationed and out-of-pocket (WHO). (6) Self-care gap: most attention and products target mobility in a narrow sense; the combined need of mobility + self-care + essential daily activities (e.g., pressure management, transfers, toileting, eating) is addressed by fragmented products, not by one locally buildable system. (7) Data gap (honest limitation): a single authoritative per-unit US-dollar price for a standard wheelchair in low-income countries was not retrievable in this run (WHO's 2022 Global Report on Assistive Technology is the primary source; its PDF was inaccessible, and the WHO news item on wheelchair pricing 404s); figures cited come from WHO's own assistive-technology fact sheet, which reports the same Global Report statistics.

What a solution must do

  • Affordability at scale: unit cost of materials/production low enough that provision is comparable to the benchmark of scaling ear and hearing care at <US$1.40 per person per year of system investment (WHO's stated global cost target); a candidate whose per-unit materials cost is an order of magnitude above the price of a basic donated wheelchair fails
  • Durability: survive at least 1 year of continuous daily use in durability testing without structural breakdown - the bar at which the foam cushions currently used in Brazil failed in fatigue testing (Guimaraes & Mann 2003)
  • Function: measurably move users out of housebound isolation - e.g., the donated-wheelchair benchmark is +11% independence and health/mood improvement within 12 months (Shore & Juillerat 2012); a candidate must show an equivalent or better functional gain in a pilot, not just exist
  • Safety: for seating, pressure distribution at least as good as the best locally available alternative (foam) and meeting the standard for preventing pressure sores in long-term seated use; for rolling devices, stable against tipping in the field conditions used
  • Local buildability: buildable, fit and repairable by a trained local maker using basic hand tools (cut, drill, weld or bolt, stitch) and materials available in local markets (e.g., bicycle inner tubes, sheet metal, steel tubing, plastic, fabric) - no imported precision components as single points of failure
  • No-dependency: usable without grid electricity and without internet/cloud connectivity; any powered variant must have a manual fallback and batteries replaceable locally
  • Maintainability: all wear parts replaceable with locally purchasable materials; a written open instruction set that a non-engineer can follow; repair documented and demonstrable by local users
  • Fit and appropriateness: size-adjustable or fabricable for the local population's anthropometrics and conditions (tropical heat, dust, rain, unpaved surfaces) - addressing WHO's finding that charity/donated products are frequently inappropriate for the user
  • Coverage of self-care, not only mobility: the design must also enable essential daily activities (eating, washing, toileting, sitting comfortably for long hours), since the need is 'moving around, self-care and carrying out essential daily activities' - a mobility-only device that ignores pressure relief, transfers and daily tasks does not meet the brief

Constraints

  • Power: target users largely have no reliable grid power - powered solutions need locally replaceable batteries and a manual fallback
  • Tools and skills: local workshops have basic hand tools; skilled rehabilitation workforce is scarce - fewer than 10 skilled rehabilitation practitioners per 1 million population in many LMIC settings (WHO) - so the build procedure must be executable without specialist orthotics expertise and training must be short (days, not months)
  • Materials: must be sourced in local markets (tubing, sheet metal, plastic, fabric, bicycle parts); supply chains for imported AT parts are unreliable - procurement challenges and inadequate product range are named WHO barriers
  • Climate: tropical heat, humidity, dust, rain and unpaved terrain - materials must be corrosion- and rot-resistant; the local durability test bar is 1+ year of daily use
  • Connectivity: low or no internet - no cloud-dependent calibration, updates or support; instructions and repairs must work offline
  • Cost of care context: households with persons with disabilities have constrained resources (higher poverty, lower employment, food insecurity - World Bank); the solution must not impose new recurring out-of-pocket payments the household cannot absorb
  • Scale: demand is 2.5B+ people growing to 3.5B by 2050 (WHO) while AT markets are small, underdeveloped and fragmented - the design must be reproducible by many independent local makers without a central factory
