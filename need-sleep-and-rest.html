The need

A broad, cross-cutting population, concentrated in four groups with named numbers. (1) General adults: ~30.5% of US adults sleep <7 hours on average in 2024 (men 30.6%, women 30.4%) and insomnia disorder affects ~12.4% of the adult population pooled across 47 true-random-sample studies (95% CI 9.0-16.8%; ~16.3% by self-report) - i.e. roughly 1 in 8 adults has clinical insomnia and ~1 in 3 sleeps too little. (2) Workers: >1/3 of the global workforce regularly works >48 hours/week and ~9% work 55+ hours/week, with the burden concentrated in men (72% of related deaths) and in the Western Pacific and South-East Asia regions, middle-aged/older. (3) School-age adolescents: biologically shift to a late sleep phase during puberty and need 9-10 hours/night, yet many start middle/high school before 8:30 AM. (4) People exposed to night noise: WHO recommends less than 30 dB(A) inside bedrooms at night for good-quality sleep and less than 40 dB(A) of annual average (Lnight) outside bedrooms to prevent adverse effects; in the EU, ~40% of the population is exposed to road-traffic noise above 55 dB(A) and more than 30% at night, and the less-affluent - who cannot afford quiet residential areas or adequately insulated homes - are hit disproportionately. Those in dense urban areas, near roads/airports, and in poorly insulated informal housing are most likely to exceed these limits. Settings are the workplace, the household bedroom, and the school day.

Severe, chronic, and rising - not a transient inconvenience. Working 55+ hours/week carries an estimated 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from ischaemic heart disease versus 35-40 hours/week (WHO/ILO 2021). That exposure is already lethal at scale: 745,000 deaths worldwide in 2016 (398,000 stroke, 347,000 heart disease) were attributed to 55+ hour weeks, a 29% increase since 2000, and the share of the population working that long is still growing (9% globally). Chronic sleep insufficiency is also linked to cognitive impairment, mood disturbance, and higher risk of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. Insomnia is persistent/recurrent by definition (DSM-5: difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep 3+ nights/week for 3+ months) with a ~12.4% pooled adult prevalence. The economic harm is persistent too: up to US$411 billion per year in the US alone from insufficient sleep (about 1.23 million lost working days/yr), projected to grow. Adolescent sleep restriction recurs every school night and is driven by the biology of puberty, so it is chronic rather than episodic.

What people do now

Three current responses, each with real cost. (1) Clinical - CBT-I (cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia) is the guideline-recommended first-line treatment for chronic insomnia (AASM 2021, STRONG recommendation, benefits strongly outweigh short-term effects), but it is delivered by a small, specialized pool of trained sleep-medicine therapists/psychologists, is expensive, has long wait times, and is unavailable to most of the ~12.4% affected; no open, low-cost, self-administered equivalent reaches the majority. (2) Policy - medical bodies (AASM 2014/2017) recommend delaying middle/high school start times to 8:30 AM or later, and WHO/ILO (2021) call on governments, employers, and workers to agree working-hour limits, but adoption is patchy because it depends on individual school boards, national legislation, and employer negotiation - no household-level lever exists. (3) Environmental - WHO (2018) sets a <40 dB(A) Lnight outdoor bedroom-noise limit (<30 dB(A) indoors for good sleep), but meeting it requires urban, transport, and building-planning action, not individual action; people who cannot afford quiet housing or adequate insulation self-medicate instead, and noise-driven sleep loss drives up medical visits and spending on sleeping pills (a recurring household cost, per WHO). The measured cost of leaving this unmet: up to US$411B/yr (US) in lost productivity and ~745,000 preventable deaths/yr globally from long-hours work.

Where existing solutions fall short

The strongest, most evidence-based interventions do not reach the people who need them. CBT-I is the first-line treatment for insomnia (AASM 2021, STRONG) but is delivered by scarce, expensive specialists - there is no open, low-cost, self-administered version that reaches the ~12.4% of adults with insomnia. The three largest documented drivers of unmet sleep/rest - long working hours (745,000 deaths/yr at 55+ h/wk), early school start times, and night noise above WHO's <40 dB(A) bedroom limit - are addressed only by policy, school boards, and urban planning; no household-level, locally buildable intervention mitigates them, so shift workers, early-start students, and residents near roads/airports in poor housing remain exposed. Commercial sleep products (mattresses, blackout curtains, white-noise machines, smart sleep trackers) are either costly, power/connectivity dependent, or treat symptoms rather than the underlying physical conditions (noise, light, temperature, space) - and none are open, cheap, and locally reproducible. Finally, the evidence base is concentrated in high-income, well-housed populations (US CDC data, European noise guidelines), leaving few validated baselines for sleep conditions in informal or low-income housing and in the Global South, so locally buildable solutions currently lack a cheap, standard way to prove they work in those settings.

What a solution must do

  • Improves a physical sleep-environment condition for a sleeping adult or adolescent - specifically night noise, light/glare, or room temperature - with a measurable target: e.g., reduce the sound level reaching the sleeper below WHO's bedroom night-noise threshold (<40 dB(A) Lnight outside, lower inside) or cut interior light to blackout level (<1 lux) without requiring the occupant to change behavior.
  • Costs under US$100 in materials for a complete per-room unit, and a core passive element (e.g., a noise/light barrier) under US$50 in materials, so it is affordable for low-income and informal-housing households (context: insufficient sleep already costs the US up to US$411B/yr).
  • Buildable by a local, semi-skilled maker from written documentation in a few hours using common hand tools (saw, drill, screwdriver, basic fasteners/sewing); the core function must not depend on laser cutting, 3D printing, CNC, or imported-only components.
  • Operates passively and requires no grid electricity and no internet connectivity to deliver its main benefit (a battery or low-power option is acceptable, but power and connectivity are optional, not required).
  • Is safe in a sleeping space: no sharp edges, no open fire or heat source that can overheat a room, no added noise source, low flammability and minimal chemical off-gassing (VOCs) from materials in close nightly contact with sleepers, and stable for years of daily use.
  • Fits small bedrooms and shared/informal housing: modular or adaptable to different room and window sizes, and usable in both hot and cold climates without making the sleeping space uncomfortably warm or cold.
  • Demonstrably improves a measurable sleep-recovery outcome - e.g., enables the recommended sleep duration (>=7h for adults, >=9-10h for adolescents) or reduces a standard self-reported sleep-disturbance score (ISI or PSQI) in a small pre/post or paired test - so the effect is verifiable rather than asserted.
  • Is fully open: published with a complete bill of materials, dimensions, step-by-step build instructions, and test/acceptance criteria, so any community can reproduce it without proprietary parts or licenses.
  • Addresses at least one of the named, documented drivers (noise, light, temperature, or space) that a household can control, rather than relying on changes to working hours or school start times (which are policy, not local builds).

Constraints

  • Materials: must use items obtainable in a local hardware store or market (fabric, wood/bamboo, foam or other basic insulation, seals, fasteners); avoid specialized, imported-only, or single-source components.
  • Tools: buildable with common household/hand tools; the core function should not require laser cutting, 3D printing, or CNC (these may be optional community conveniences, not dependencies).
  • Skills: an unskilled or semi-skilled maker should be able to build from the documentation in a few hours with no specialized technical knowledge.
  • Power/connectivity: no grid power and no internet required for the core function; passive (acoustic/thermal/light-blocking) design is preferred; if electronics are used, low-power and tolerant of no connectivity.
  • Climate: must function across a realistic range of hot/cold conditions; thermal elements must not trap heat in hot climates.
  • Space: must fit small, shared, or informal dwellings and adapt to different room/window sizes; modular where possible.
  • Safety: low flammability and minimal chemical off-gassing (VOCs) from materials near sleepers; no new noise, heat, or trip hazard introduced into the bedroom.
  • Cost: total materials for a complete unit well under US$100 (passive core under US$50), so the price is below typical household disposable income for a durable good.
  • Data gap to respect: published prevalence and exposure data skew to high-income, well-housed populations (US CDC; European noise guidelines); local baselines are scarce, so the solution should be testable with low-cost local measurement (e.g., a phone or a US$10-20 sound meter and lux meter) rather than requiring clinical instrumentation.
