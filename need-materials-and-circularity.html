The need

Shelter: 2.8 billion people suffer some form of housing inadequacy (UN-Habitat 2023); up to 3.4 billion lack secure, safe, adequate housing including more than 1 billion in informal settlements/slums per the UN-Habitat World Cities Report 2026 summary; 1 billion slum dwellers in 2020, 90% in Africa or Asia; 300-318 million homeless. Water/sanitation: 2.1 billion lack safely managed drinking water and 3.4 billion lack safely managed sanitation (2024, WHO/UNICEF JMP), with fragile contexts 33-38 percentage points worse and rural sanitation coverage at only 49%. Digital/electronic materials: the entire connected population depends on electronics that become 62 million tonnes of e-waste per year (ITU, 2022). Scale driver: 96,000 new affordable and accessible housing units are needed every day to 2030 (UN-Habitat) — each unit material-intensive.

Persistent and worsening, not episodic. The absolute slum population grew by 165 million over 20 years and the decline rate is slowing (UN-Habitat 2023); the world is explicitly 'failing to provide adequate housing' at the SDG midpoint. Poor housing is associated with respiratory and cardiovascular disease, mental health conditions, infectious disease transmission, injury, and increased health-care costs (Lancet Public Health, 2025; WHO Housing and Health Guidelines). The enabler's own footprint is a top-three climate driver: buildings and construction consume 32% of global energy and 34% of global CO2, and the materials (cement, steel) are responsible for 18% of global emissions (UNEP/GlobalABC 2024/25). Waste streams compound this: 62 million tonnes of e-waste/yr with 77.7% not properly recycled; 353 million tonnes of plastic waste/yr with 91% not recycled and 22% leaking to environment; municipal solid waste already 2.56 billion tonnes/yr and outpacing management capacity (ITU; OECD; World Bank).

What people do now

The world builds almost exclusively with high-embodied-carbon materials: cement and steel production alone is 18% of global CO2 emissions (UNEP/GlobalABC). The poorest self-build from whatever is cheapest locally — often the same cement/steel/tin, or informal salvaged materials — with no formal data on typical cost, and 96,000 units/day of new demand are unmet (UN-Habitat). Recycling is minimal: only 22.3% of e-waste is properly collected and recycled, and countries without any e-waste legislation have collection/recycling rates close to zero versus 25% where legislation exists (ITU); only 9% of plastic waste is recycled, 50% goes to landfill, 22% is mismanaged/leaks, and recycled plastics are still only ~6% of total plastics production (OECD 2022). Global waste management cost USD 252 billion directly in 2020 (USD 361 billion including pollution, health and climate externalities), heading to USD 640.3 billion by 2050 without action; a full circular-economy model would instead yield a net gain of USD 108.5 billion/year (UNEP GWMO 2024). Waste generation (2.56 billion tonnes in 2022, to 3.86 billion by 2050) is outpacing both local system capacity and municipal budgets (World Bank What a Waste 3.0).

Where existing solutions fall short

Existing formal construction is locked to high-embodied-carbon materials (cement/steel/aluminium = 18% of global CO2; the sector 34% of global CO2 and 32% of energy — UNEP/GlobalABC) and linear disposal. Recycled-content markets are structurally weak: recycled plastics are only ~6% of production and 'viewed as substitutes for virgin plastic' (OECD); only 22.3% of e-waste is properly recycled despite containing USD 91 billion of metals, and recycling collapses to near zero without legislation (ITU); low- and middle-income countries face an estimated EUR 25 billion/year of unmet waste-management infrastructure investment (OECD); C&D waste is a multi-billion-tonne stream mostly of concrete, brick and glass that is rarely reprocessed (UN Zero Waste brief). WASH spending is concentrated in new infrastructure rather than local materials, repair capacity, and reuse. No open, locally buildable material system exists that simultaneously: uses local low-embodied-carbon inputs, meets WHO housing-health and WASH standards, is designed for deconstruction/reuse with local salvage markets, and operates without municipal waste infrastructure, regulation, or reliable supply chains — i.e., the conditions under which the 2.8-3.4 billion people lacking adequate housing and the 2.1-3.4 billion lacking safe water/sanitation actually live.

What a solution must do

  • Shelter + health: building systems built from locally available, low-cost materials (e.g., earth, bamboo, brick, recycled aggregate) whose cost per unit of floor area is at or below prevailing local self-build cost, and which meet WHO Housing and Health Guidelines standards — efficient thermal insulation, safe low and high indoor temperatures, indoor air quality, and home-injury safety (smoke/CO protection) (Lancet Public Health; UN-Habitat).
  • Shelter at scale: material supply must be reproducible for at least 96,000 new affordable units per day by 2030 (UN-Habitat), using inputs procurable within a local region, with the lowest feasible share of cement and steel — the materials responsible for 18% of global CO2 emissions (UNEP/GlobalABC).
  • Water + sanitation: durable, repairable WASH components (pipes, storage tanks, latrine/structure materials) for the 2.1 billion without safely managed drinking water and 3.4 billion without safely managed sanitation (WHO/UNICEF JMP), at a cost within low-income household reach and a service life long enough to amortize (design-for-20-years, repairable with locally available tools).
  • Shelter + local economy (circularity): design for deconstruction — components and materials recoverable at end-of-life and re-entered into new builds, creating a local salvage market that lowers construction material costs and creates livelihoods, as documented in the UN SG Zero Waste Advisory Board construction-waste brief; measurable as a landfill-diversion target of 65% or higher for C&D debris, a rate already enforced as policy (San Francisco ordinance).
  • Electronics/digital needs: repair-first design and local recycling pathways for the 62 million tonnes/yr of e-waste, recovering the secondary metals in it — 31 billion kg of metals worth USD 91 billion (incl. USD 19 billion copper, USD 15 billion gold) went into 2022's e-waste, and formal recycling already avoids 900 million tonnes of primary ore and 93 million tonnes CO2e per year (ITU/Global E-waste Monitor 2024); safe processing must not depend on long-distance export logistics.
  • Water + food + environment: reduce single-use plastics in packaging and distribution of essentials, or provide local recycling where none exists — 22% of the 353 million tonnes of annual plastic waste currently leaks into terrestrial or aquatic environments, contaminating the water systems these needs depend on (OECD).
  • Climate (all needs): low-embodied-carbon material alternatives measurably below cement/steel — e.g., bamboo as a structural reinforcement alternative (UN Zero Waste brief) and recycled steel, whose recycling avoids about 950 million tonnes of CO2 and 1 billion tonnes of iron ore per year; spec in kg CO2e per m2 of floor area or per functional unit (UNEP/GlobalABC; UN Zero Waste brief).

Constraints

  • Local sourcing is mandatory: materials or their inputs must be obtainable within a local region — long-distance transport of cement/steel is the dominant cost and carbon failure mode for remote and rural sites, and 96,000 units/day of new demand (UN-Habitat) cannot be met with imported inputs.
  • Must function where municipal infrastructure is absent: waste management capacity already cannot keep up with 2.56 billion tonnes/yr of generation (World Bank), and 50% of plastic waste already ends up in landfill with 22% leaked (OECD) — so recycling/reuse solutions must not presuppose existing collection, treatment, or landfill systems.
  • Must work in low-regulation environments: many countries have no e-waste legislation and correspondingly near-zero recycling rates (ITU); a solution that waits for EPR or landfill-diversion policy to be adopted first will not reach those populations.
  • Must include informal-sector workers: UNEP explicitly calls for a just transition for informal waste workers; exclusion of existing informal recyclers/pickers makes circular systems socially and politically unviable (UNEP GWMO 2024).
  • Maintenance without grid or specialist skills: WASH and housing components must be maintainable with locally available tools by the end user, in areas where 2.1 billion already lack safely managed water (WHO/UNICEF JMP) and where grid/technical support is unreliable.
  • Cost floor: for the users served — the 1 billion slum dwellers, the 96,000 units/day gap, the 2.1 billion without safe water — affordability, security of tenure, and suitability are the WHO/Lancet three-pillar test; a material system that meets quality standards but not the cost pillar does not serve the need (Lancet Public Health).
