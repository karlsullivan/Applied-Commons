The canonical, most-adopted open face shield design from Prusa Research, with community fast-print guides and slicer settings (e.g. yschroeder's repo). For medical and general-purpose splash/spatter protection; mature, low-cost, single-part 3D print.

Testing

CheckHowPass if
Splash/droplet impact protection (core need: healthcare, dental, home-care splash and spatter)EN 166:2001 test suite executed by an officially designated lab (Prusa's certification included an official laser impact test); required for anyone manufacturing for the EU market under PPE Regulation (EU) 2016/425. Certification is per-manufacturer — Prusa's RC3 CE certificate (14 May 2020) does not transfer to other producers (OBL license is the shortcut).Pass EN 166:2001. Certified reference points: RC3 CE-certified for frontal protection against flying drops; PRUSA PRO (RC4) meets EN 166:2001 protection class 3 for drops and sprays (marking '1 S 3' class on the shop PRUSA PRO).
Headband + chin structural integrity (home-printed unit)yschroeder community QC bend suite on the finished PETG part: (1) press the plastic-sheet tabs down on a table; (2) bend the rubber-band hooks all the way to each other; (3) bend the two sides at 90 degrees in both directions, multiple times; (4) bend the two sides across each other. Plus visual check of the two outer perimeters and hook tops. Note: the suite is specified for the v18 fast-print; RC3/other versions are less flexible and will break earlier.Part must not crack at all during any bend (reference part printed from PETG). The two perimeters must be visibly fused; if they do not stick together, the finished part is 'extremely weak' — reject the batch.
Material suitability (thermal + mechanical, tied to sterilization reuse)Verify filament is PETG (Prusa-specified; Prusament PETG used in all verified tests) or ASA/ABS; verify any planned sterilization method's temperature against the material. Cross-check: 24 h UV-C aging reduced PLA 6-8% and PETG >30% in tensile/compressive strength (Polymers 2021); Prusa FAQ: many sterilization procedures exceed 60 C, which PLA cannot take.PETG is the default; PLA only acceptable if the disinfection path stays below ~60 C and no UV-C aging is expected.
Disinfection durability for reuseHC-FMUSP protocol (AJIC 2020): soak gauze in 70% ethanol, H2O2 + quaternary-ammonium mixture, or 0.1% sodium hypochlorite; apply 1 min, spontaneous dry; repeat up to 40 cycles; inspect visor for cracks and visibility after each phase; autoclave (121 C, 15 min) as the damage control.No physical damage to visor or headband after 40 chemical disinfection cycles. Autoclaving is a failure (appreciable damage) and must be excluded.
Disinfection efficacy (verified methods only)Use the methods verified by Czech labs on Prusament PETG shields (SYNLAB with live SARS-CoV-2 solution; UCT with E. coli, P. aeruginosa, B. cereus; LABTECH with coagulase-positive staphylococci; smear-test evaluation of active/inactive organisms): hot-air dryer 65 C / 60 min; WHO hand-rub (75% IPA) 5 min; IPA 96% 5 min; IPA 75% 5 min; household bleach min 0.01% hypochlorite 2 min+; UV-C 30 W, <280 nm, 15 min; ethanol 70-80% 5 min; H2O2 25% 5 min. Submerge parts or spray at least twice.Bacteria and virus inactivation per the lab smear tests. Soap + warm water is a fallback (2 min wash, repeat, dry) but is NOT a verified method — prefer the table.
Fit and compatibility with secondary PPE (need brief: adjunct to mask/respirator)Wear test with the actual wearer: shield over surgical mask, respirator, and/or glasses (RC2+ geometry moved the visor further from the forehead specifically to fit over respirators and goggles); Prusa's guidance for small local production is to 'test the shield's functionality in cooperation with the person who is going to wear it'.No interference with the mask/respirator seal, no pressure points at the temple (RC2/RC3 headband prints uncompressed), wearer confirms frontal coverage for flying drops. Shield alone is never sufficient — always combined with mask/respirator.
Visor specificationMeasure the sheet against Prusa's published specs: clear PETG (e.g. Covestro Vivak) 0.5-0.8 mm (0.02-0.04 in), 240x240 mm standard or 240x310 mm extended (dental), holes punched per the published template PDF with a standard office punch; confirm the chin ('lower part') is present — it is a vital structural part and must not be skipped.Sheet matches thickness/size spec; all punched holes align; chin piece installed.

Failure modes

FailureEffectMitigation
Gaps between the two outer perimeters (fast-print v10/v18, 0% infill, 2-wall construction)Finished part is 'extremely weak'; gaps lead to crumbling under load (documented in yschroeder QC video and photos).Increase external-perimeter extrusion width (above 0.87 mm; 1.0 mm worked in the author's testing) or increase extrusion multiplier so the two walls fuse; if you cannot get the walls to stick together, print the original RC3 or v10 instead.
Holes in the part (especially hook tops)Reduced structural strength, cosmetic defects.Increase external-perimeter extrusion width (1.0 mm); reduce solid/top solid infill extrusion width to 0.55 mm or less; raise solid-layer count to close top holes; use the 'Lily' variant if layer adhesion fails.
PLA used instead of PETG for headband/chinSoftens/deforms at sterilization temperatures above 60 C; more brittle under impact; 6-8% property loss after 24 h UV-C.Print in PETG (Prusa-specified, all verified tests used PETG); if PETG is unavailable use ASA or ABS; restrict disinfection to <60 C methods if PLA is unavoidable.
Repeated/over-dosed UV-C or alcohol sterilizationPETG loses >30% tensile/compressive strength after 24 h UV-C aging; repeated sterilizing alcohol reduced PETG tensile strength ~20% (Grzelak et al., cited in Polymers 2021) — headband weakens over its service life.Use the verified method table at stated doses (UV-C 15 min); prefer short chemical cycles; plan for reprint when the part shows any crack in the bend QC.
Fogging of the visor (breath condensation)Blocks visibility during care (shower assistance, procedures); ~70% of PSWs/nurses in a VHA survey said fogging makes safe care harder; the common response — wiping mid-procedure with glove/alcohol wipe/towel — smears the surface and introduces contamination.Surfactant film: VHA field-tested Live Clean baby shampoo or Dawn dish soap, applied and dried, reduced fogging for about 2-4 clients per treatment; acclimatize the shield to room temperature before entering a warm room; seal the mask's upper edge with tape to redirect breath; never wipe mid-procedure — replace the shield.
Wrong visor material/geometry: A4/888-punch sheets, laser-printer sheets, or thin laminating filmUsers reported headaches and vertigo after longer use; laminating film has bad optical properties; the sheet does not bend properly around the part with 0.8 mm stock; 297 mm wide 0.8 mm PETG is too heavy/uncomfortable; fine work (dentistry, suturing) becomes difficult. yschroeder explicitly warns against these substitutes.Use the standard 240x240 / 240x310 mm 0.5-0.8 mm PETG sheet with the published hole pattern; if no laser cutter is available, a thick (shape-holding) laminating foil or a cut 2 L PET bottle is the documented fallback, not A4 film.
Autoclaving (steam sterilization) of a printed shieldAppreciable damage to headband and visor after 121 C/15 min (HC-FMUSP study).Do not autoclave; use the verified chemical/heat table (hot air 65 C/60 min, alcohols, dilute bleach, H2O2, UV-C 15 min).
Alcohol vapor on donning after disinfectionEye redness at 1 minute after 70% alcohol disinfection (HC-FMUSP physician/technician exposure test).Wait 3-5 minutes after disinfection before wearing; ensure the part is dry (spontaneous drying) before use.
Non-manifold/defective STL in slicer (community field report on RC3 file)Slicer fills the gap between front and rear band, producing a distorted print.Repair the mesh before slicing (community fix with Netfabb, reported in the Prusa blog comment thread); verify the part against the published QC bend suite after printing.
Sharp points left by high-speed farm printing (RC2-era, noted in RC3 changelog)Comfort/safety hazard at the temple and edges.De-burr before handover; RC3 removed the hexagonal holes that caused the venting-related sharp points; inspect every unit visually before assembly.

Known gaps

What the original design does not document:

  • No official per-unit BOM exists: filament weight is published only for the PRUSA PRO headband (45 g); chin-part weight, visor sheet area/mass, and elastic band dimensions/price are not documented upstream
  • Visor sheet supplier and price not stated (only the material class '0.5 mm PETG sheet (Covestro VIVAK)' / 'any clear laser cuttable plastic with similar thickness')
  • Original 'Prusament PETG Orange for PPE' 1 kg discounted SKU page now returns 404 (discontinued), so the PPE filament price is not retrievable; standard Prusament PETG used as reference
  • No per-unit build cost published by Prusa; the only all-in figure is MakerNexus ~$5/unit (2020, US, donation-funded)
  • Elastic band specification (width, length, hole diameter) not documented - only 'elastic band with holes' and the >=10 mm end-cut advice
  • Visor yield per sheet not documented for EU/A4 (US design fits 11x8.5 in sheets; part-per-sheet count not stated)
  • Laser-cutter access/cost not documented; hand-cut and PET-bottle fallbacks are community-sourced, not official
  • Canonical prusaresearch.com/faceshield page and the Prusa KB FAQ body could not be retrieved in this run (timeouts / navigation-only extraction); content is represented only via other retrieved official pages
  • Canonical Prusa RC3/RC3.1/PRUSA-PRO headband + shield STL/3MF files are hosted only on Printables (models 25857/27267/32714/32810/27950); Printables has no public file API and per-file download URLs are session/Cloudflare-gated, so no commit-pinned raw link exists (the project is not distributed as a git repo). The only direct-download visor files are the prusa3d.com PDFs and the one media.printables.com CE3 DXF indexed here.
  • No parametric CAD source (OpenSCAD/STEP/FreeCAD) for the canonical Prusa design — Prusa publishes only final STL/3MF slices; the only parametric source is the MakerNexus remix (prusa-covid-shield-remix.scad + .f3z), a modification not the upstream.
  • No schematic / PCB / firmware: the device is passive (printed headband + sheet visor + rubber band), so these categories are N/A, not missing — but Prusa documents no electronics at all.
  • yschroeder/face-shield has no declared licence file (GitHub API license=null; README is a custom AS-IS 'not a medical device' disclaimer) — SPDX id cannot be assigned.
  • Prusa files carry a non-commercial licence with no SPDX identifier; the exact legal terms are on the model pages but not machine-readable.
  • maker-nexus wiki page (makernexuswiki.com) and manual.prusa3d.com were unreachable from this host (DNS/route error) and could not be indexed.
  • US-version visor PDF (COVID19_SHIELD_VISOR_US_1PC.pdf) is referenced on model 27950 but its direct URL was not independently verified from this host.
  • No BOM/cost for the sheet, rubber band or punch is documented upstream (materials are user-supplied; Prusa only names '0.5 mm PETG (Covestro VIVAK)' as what they used).
  • No quantitative mechanical test specification for home-printed units: the yschroeder QC doc states explicitly 'we can't really tell you what your printed part needs to withstand' — it is a 5-bend demo with a PETG reference, version-specific (v18 only), with no force, cycle count, or failure threshold. No published bend-cycle or impact test spec exists for FDM home production.
  • No splash/droplet protection test data for home-printed units — EN 166 results apply only to Prusa's certified production (and the certification is per-manufacturer).
  • No head-to-head comparison of 3D-printed shields vs commercial injection-molded shields (need brief flags the same: 'still no head-to-head vs commercial shields').
  • No fogging-resistance test of the design itself; the design has no factory anti-fog option and upstream documents no anti-fog performance (third-party workarounds only: VHA, CliniEco).
  • No published optical-clarity/transmittance test of the visor (visibility is only checked qualitatively — 'crack and visibility examined' after disinfection cycles).
  • No published reuse-cycle lifetime: the FAQ still labels shields single-use while the disinfection article documents verified reuse methods and 40-cycle durability; no maximum-cycle guidance is given. Prusa KB says its results were still evolving at publication.
  • No fit-testing across head sizes or comfort study from upstream; fit evidence is the design changelog (RC2/RC3 geometry changes) and the wear-test recommendation, plus third-party surveys (e.g. OMFS survey reporting ~74% felt shields reduced surgical efficiency).
  • The EN 166:2001 standard text itself is not openly retrievable; its specific test procedures (impact mass/velocity for class 3, optical requirements) could not be verified from open sources — only its application to the RC3/PRUSA PRO via Prusa's certification write-up.

Safety

Building the shield is trivially safe and the design is mature (Prusa has donated ~200,000+ shields; disinfection methods were verified by Czech labs on Prusament PETG), so there is no do-not-build basis. Two clusters drive the verdict. First, biological-medical: a home-printed shield carries no transferable EN 166 / EN ISO 16321 protection (certification is per-manufacturer), so over-reliance in medical use is the dominant risk, controlled by adjunct-only use plus certified PPE for aerosol work. Second, the reuse and fabrication steps (chemical disinfectants, UV-C, laser cutting) and low-level electrical/fire risk from an unattended print farm. No mains-critical, battery, gas-combustion, pressure, water-food, structural, or RF hazard materially applies.

HazardSeverityControls
Over-reliance on a non-certified barrier. Home-printed units carry no verified splash protection: EN 166 / EN ISO 16321 conformity is per-manufacturer and does not transfer to a community or home print (the yschroeder repo states the design is 'not a medical device ... neither certified nor fit for any purpose'). If a builder or user treats the shield as a certified barrier, or as a substitute for a mask/respirator, a wearer or patient can be exposed to droplets/aerosols and infected.highTreat the shield as an adjunct only; always combine with a surgical mask or respirator - it is never a replacement for one.; Do not rely on a home-printed unit in high-risk / aerosol-generating medical procedures; use certified PPE there.; Wear-test each unit with the actual wearer (over mask/respirator/goggles; no temple pressure; confirm frontal coverage) before handover.; Disclose the build environment to recipients and that a home print is not independently certified.; For EU-market placement or large-scale/clinical production: obtain notified-body conformity assessment (own type-examination, or OBL 'consent to use of test results' via the original notified body, e.g. ITC Zlin) plus an EU Declaration of Conformity - this step requires a qualified/certified process
Cross-contamination during production and cleaning. Parts handled on a home/farm print farm, and used shields (potentially contaminated on every surface, including after a direct cough/aerosol event), can re-contaminate people, surfaces, or other shields if hygiene is not controlled.mediumWhen making shields, act as if infected: face mask + a fresh pair of gloves per batch; store finished parts immediately in sealable bags; disclose the manufacturing environment to recipients.; When cleaning a used shield: the cleaner wears gloves, a mask and (ideally) another shield; disinfect the work surface first; disassemble (band, chin, headband) before cleaning; never clean multiple shields at once.; If a shield was hit directly by aerosol (e.g. a cough): discard to hazardous waste if a spare exists; if it is the only one, wash thoroughly under running water, disinfect the sink, and alert colleagues.
Reuse disinfection uses 75-96% isopropanol/ethanol, household bleach (sodium hypochlorite, min 0.01%), and 25% hydrogen peroxide: skin/eye irritation and burns, bleach fumes; and mixing bleach with acids/ammonia/other cleaners can release chlorine gas.mediumWear gloves and a mask (and ideally a spare shield) when handling; work in ventilation.; Never mix bleach with other disinfectants or cleaners (chlorine gas risk).; Use only the lab-verified methods at the stated concentrations/doses (IPA 75-96% 5 min; ethanol 70-80% 5 min; bleach min 0.01% 2 min+; H2O2 25% 5 min); submerge or spray at least twice; spot-test near the visor edge first.; After an alcohol wipe, let it dry and wait 3-5 minutes before donning (vapor at ~1 min can cause eye redness); do not autoclave (121 C / 15 min damages the part).
UV-C disinfection (<280 nm): direct eye/skin exposure causes photokeratitis/erythema; over-dosing degrades PETG (the build pack cites >30% tensile/compressive strength loss at 24 h), weakening the headband over its service life.mediumUse UV-C only at the verified dose (30 W, <280 nm, 15 min); do not over-dose.; Interlock/cover the chamber; never view the UV-C source directly; keep eyes and skin out of the beam.; Reprint the part if any crack appears in the QC bend test (UV-C / alcohol aging weakens PETG).
Laser cutting the visor sheet (build-pack BOM lists 'laser cutter access'): laser-beam eye hazard, ignition of the PETG sheet, and polymer fumes.mediumUse an interlocked/enclosed laser; eye protection matched to the laser class; no direct beam exposure.; Fume extraction/ventilation; keep flammable stock clear of the beam; do not cut unattended; trained operator.; If no laser is available, fall back to a documented non-laser visor (thick shape-holding laminating foil or a cut 2 L PET bottle) rather than A4/laser-printer film.
Unattended FDM printing (hot nozzle/bed ~230-250 C for PETG), especially a multi-printer farm running for hours/days: low fire risk and hot-surface burns.lowUse grounded appliances; keep printers clear of flammables; avoid leaving a large farm unattended for extended periods where feasible; keep a fire extinguisher nearby.
Mains power for the 3D printers and laser cutter; a multi-printer farm wired by a non-specialist in a home/farm/village can overload circuits or be un-earthed.lowUse earthed (grounded) mains; avoid chained extension cords; one heavy load per outlet where feasible; have a qualified person wire any permanent multi-printer/laser setup.

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • Prusa PRO Face Shield (RC4) is CE-certified per EN 166:2001 protection class 3 (drops/sprays), optical class 1, 'S' increased toughness; EU Type-Examination Certificate No. 20 0247 T/NB. RC3 received CE on 14 May 2020; ~200,000 units were printed and donated in the Czech Republic.
  • The community fast-print 'v18' headband (1.67 mm constant wall, yschroeder's redesign) has no EN 166:2001 impact/drop test of any kind; its only quality control is qualitative hand-bending (press the sheet tabs down, bend the elastic hooks to 90 degrees in both directions, bend the sides across).
  • The v18 author (yschroeder) states the part is 'neither certified nor fit for any purpose' and that 'we can't really tell you what your printed part needs to withstand' - there is no measured impact-velocity or particle-size boundary for the 1.67 mm headband in any public source retrieved.
  • Controlled 24 h sterilizing UV-C aging of MEX-printed (FDM) parts reduced tensile/compressive strength of PLA by only 6-8% but PETG by over 30%; UV-C is therefore comparatively mild on a PLA headband and harsh on a PETG sheet/band, and it only affects the exposed surface of a porous print.
  • (a) FIT: RC3/RC4 fit was iterated from user feedback — headband no longer printed compressed (lower temple pressure), visor moved further from the forehead so it fits over bigger respirators and goggles, and RC4 added shoulder cutouts for comfort; headband is injection-molded (smooth, no layer lines) and adjustable via rubber band. Prusa states the visor is suitable for long-term wear even while wearing protective glasses 'no fogging'.
  • Certification is per-manufacturer, not per-design: a clinic reproducing Prusa shields in volume must run its own notified-body process (~3000-5000+ EUR, ~3 months, 20+ sample units) or take Prusa's OBL route (~700 EUR, Prusa covers it for EU entities, requires identical materials/process). This is a real hidden cost/delay for the printed route at 1000-unit scale in an EU jurisdiction; the commercial route carries its CE already.
  • 'RC3/RC4 are certified against EN 166' is an overstated premise: the Prusa community RC3/RC4 headbands are the same open-source design and carry the explicit 'neither certified nor fit for any purpose' disclaimer; no EN 166 certification is granted to community prints.
  • Field reports show the shields were used as splash/droplet PPE, not high-velocity impact PPE: MakerNexus (Prusa RC3-based design) fielded 250,000 face shields to Bay Area hospitals with no EN 166 impact testing reported; Prusa printed and donated ~200,000 shields in the Czech Republic. Use context is aerosol/droplet splash protection, which is consistent with the class-3 splash rating, not the impact (A/B) mark.
