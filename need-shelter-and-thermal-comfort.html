The need

Roughly 2.1 billion people worldwide cook on open fires or inefficient stoves on kerosene, biomass or coal; 923 million of them are in sub-Saharan Africa (WHO, 2022). 1.16 billion people - about one in four urban residents - live in slums or informal settlements, concentrated in Central/Southern Asia (334M), Eastern/Southeastern Asia (362M) and sub-Saharan Africa (265M), with SSA the highest share of urban population in slums at 53.6%; projections put SSA up by another 360M slum dwellers by 2030 (UN SDG 11). Fewer than 1 in 10 people globally lack access to electricity, and three-quarters of that population is in sub-Saharan Africa (OWID/IEA). 49% of the global rural population still relies on polluting fuels for cooking versus 14% urban (WHO). The affected populations are overwhelmingly poor households in low- and middle-income countries, rural areas, and informal urban settlements, with women and children bearing the largest health burden from exposure.

The harm is severe, chronic, and fatal at scale. Household air pollution caused an estimated 2.9 million deaths per year in 2021 (over 309,000 of them children under 5) and 95 million DALYs; combined with ambient pollution it accounts for 6.7 million premature deaths per year (WHO). Non-optimal ambient temperatures are associated with 5.08 million deaths per year globally - 8.52% of all deaths are cold-related and 0.91% heat-related - with Sub-Saharan Africa having the highest cold-related excess death rate (Zhao et al 2021, Lancet Planetary Health). Heat-related mortality for people over 65 rose ~85% between 2000-2004 and 2017-2021; 2003's European heatwave killed an estimated 70,000 people and 2022's 61,672 (WHO). WHO projects 250,000 additional deaths per year from 2030-2050 from malnutrition, malaria, diarrhoea and heat stress alone, plus US$2-4 billion per year in direct health damage by 2030. In poorly ventilated dwellings, indoor smoke can reach particle levels 100x above acceptable. The deficit is persistent: at current rates only 78% of the global population is expected to have clean cooking by 2030, leaving ~1.8 billion still without.

What people do now

Most affected households cook and heat with open fires or inefficient stoves burning wood, dung, crop waste, charcoal, coal or kerosene, in poorly ventilated dwellings; fuel gathering and cooking labor falls predominantly on women and children. Housing in the 1.16B-person slum/informal-settlement population is typically self-built with whatever material is on hand (metal sheeting, tin roofs, corrugated materials, mud, thatch), with no running water, shared toilets, little waste management, and overcrowding. Low-cost passive thermal management is the norm: opening windows at night for cooling, closing and shading windows by day, using fans (only effective below 40 degC per WHO guidance), wet cloth and showers, light clothing, shade. The cost is high: the global average housing cost burden is 31% of household income, rising to 43.5% in sub-Saharan Africa (UN SDG 11, 2024-25 data); in countries below ~US$2,000 GDP per capita, fewer than 10% of households have access to clean cooking fuels (OWID); and in sub-Saharan Africa, 49% of rural households remain on polluting fuels (WHO). People effectively pay in labor time (fuel gathering), income (housing cost burden), and health (millions of deaths).

Where existing solutions fall short

Existing solutions cluster in three categories, and each leaves a gap. (1) Formal construction (concrete, brick, steel-frame) meets weather protection and thermal comfort but fails on cost, buildability, and land-tenure for the 1.16B-person slum population. (2) Cheap self-built housing (metal sheeting, tin, thatch, mud) is locally buildable and cheap but fails on thermal comfort - metal roofs amplify heat, WHO notes informal settlements are often hotter than surrounding urban areas due to building materials - and on indoor air quality because cooking still happens on open fires in poorly ventilated dwellings. (3) Mechanical HVAC and grid-based cooking/electrification meet both thermal comfort and air quality but assume grid power that three-quarters of the world's non-electrified population (in SSA) does not have, and are not locally buildable. There is a gap for a solution that is simultaneously locally buildable, sub-US$500 in materials, works without grid power, meets WHO emission targets for indoor combustion, and holds a 18-26 degC indoor band across both tropical-heat and cold-exposure climates.

What a solution must do

  • Maintain indoor operative temperature in the 18-26 degC comfort band in the target climate, measured at occupant height, across the annual heating/cooling season (reducing exposure to non-optimal temperatures associated with 5.08M deaths/yr)
  • Achieve indoor PM2.5 and CO emission rates from any combustion source at or below the WHO Guidelines for indoor air quality: household fuel combustion (2014) emission targets, so that any heating/cooking function does not re-create the 2.9M/yr household-air-pollution death burden
  • Provide weather protection against rain, wind, and solar gain such that indoor temperature stays at or below outdoor shade temperature +3 degC during the hottest hours, and at or above outdoor -3 degC during the coldest hours, without grid electricity
  • Cost under US$500 per occupable unit in materials for a household of 4-6, and total ownership cost (materials + local labor) must be less than 10% of annual household income for a household at the bottom quintile of the target country
  • Buildable with local materials and hand tools (hammer, saw, drill) in under 2 weeks by a team of 2-3 unskilled local laborers, with a bill of materials obtainable within 50 km of the build site
  • Survive the local climate for at least 10 years with annual maintenance that can be done by the household with hand tools; structural components individually replaceable
  • Provide at least 4 m2 of habitable floor area per person and a single room sealable against wind/rain; modular so that one unit can be expanded by adding another unit without demolition
  • Ventilate the occupied space at a minimum of 0.5 air changes per hour at a low pressure differential, and be compatible with a combustion-free or WHO-clean-cooking fuel (solar, LPG, biogas, electricity) for cooking/heating

Constraints

  • No reliable grid electricity in most target settings (three-quarters of the world's non-electrified population is in sub-Saharan Africa; rural access lags urban in nearly every country) - the solution must function primarily without grid power
  • No reliable supply chain or industrial fabrication; materials must be locally available (mud, thatch, timber, bamboo, stone, or shipped metal/plastic sheet) and tools limited to hand tools
  • High ambient heat load in tropical and subtropical target climates; many affected populations are chronically exposed, not just seasonally - passive-only solutions must work under sustained high temperature AND high humidity, where evaporative cooling is limited
  • Cold exposure is also a major mortality driver (8.52% of all deaths are cold-related globally, SSA highest cold excess-death rate) - a single solution cannot assume 'only hot climate'; it must be adaptable or paired across both thermal regimes
  • Limited formal skills and building codes in informal-settlement contexts; construction must be learnable from illustrated or video instructions without an engineer on site
  • Land tenure insecurity in many informal settlements - the solution should not require formal land-title processes to be erected or expanded
  • Connectivity is patchy; any instructions, monitoring, or software components must be usable offline or via low-bandwidth mobile
