The need

2.2 billion people (27% of the global population) lack a safely managed drinking-water service. Breakdown (WHO 2023): 1.5 billion with 'basic' services (improved source but >30-min round trip), 292 million with 'limited' services (>30 min to collect), 296 million drawing from unprotected wells/springs, and 115 million from untreated surface water. A further 429 million school-age children have no basic water service at school (258 million where the school has no water at all). Over 2 billion people live in water-stressed countries. The burden is concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, rural areas (80% of those without improved water are rural), low-income/informal/illegal urban settlements, and among women, girls, and children (especially under-5). Conflict-affected and climate-exposed communities are hit hardest.

Severe and persistent. 1.4 million people die each year from inadequate drinking-water, sanitation and hygiene (WHO/UNICEF JMP); unsafe water alone causes ~505,000 diarrhoeal deaths/yr, and ~1 million deaths/yr from diarrhoea due to unsafe water + sanitation + hand hygiene combined, of which 395,000 under-5 deaths are preventable. Over 251 million people required treatment for schistosomiasis (2021). The world is NOT on track: achieving universal safely managed water by 2030 requires a 6x increase in the current rate of progress (5x for sanitation, 3x for basic hygiene). Climate change, drought, and conflict are worsening scarcity and contamination, so the harm is growing, not shrinking.

What people do now

People without safely managed water either walk long distances (292 million are >30 minutes for a round trip), draw from unprotected wells/springs (296 million) or untreated surface water (115 million), or buy water. The burden is disproportionately on women, girls and children: WHO documents that long/risky journeys to collect and carry water cause musculoskeletal disorder, safety risk and time/effort lost that could be spent productively (and that children's school attendance suffers). Purchased water is often unaffordable, and WASH affordability still lacks a commonly agreed measurable threshold (UNICEF/WHO 2021). Health expenditure after waterborne illness, and stunting in children, compound the cost. In emergencies, UNICEF/WHO water trucks and pumps provide a stopgap that is not a durable solution. The gender dimension is the special focus of the JMP 2023 household report.

Where existing solutions fall short

No single open, locally buildable household technology currently closes the full pathogen + chemical gap: (1) SODIS/solar disinfection is cheap and kills bacteria but cannot reliably inactivate viruses (or protozoa) during low-sun periods, so it is climate-limited; (2) slow-sand / biosand filters give strong turbidity and E. coli removal (>90% turbidity, >3.5-log E. coli) but poor fluoride removal and incomplete protozoa inactivation; (3) chlorination kills bacteria to <1 CFU/100 mL but does not inactivate protozoa (Giardia/Cryptosporidium) and can produce disinfection byproducts at high doses; (4) no open design combines multi-barrier biological filtration + a virus/protozoa-effective disinfection step + chemical (fluoride/arsenic) removal in one low-cost, no-power, field-maintainable package. In addition, the deployment/adoption gap is real: a peer-reviewed Malawi study (2026) found that even where a technology exists (SODIS), adoption and sustainability fail without co-designed training, materials, and community ownership - so a solution that is technically correct but not contextually adopted is still a gap.

What a solution must do

  • Remove >=99.99% (4-log) of E. coli / fecal indicator bacteria from treated water (WHO health-based target; multi-barrier HWT achieves >3.5-log, target the upper bound).
  • Achieve <1 CFU/100 mL for total coliforms and E. coli in the finished/distributed water (WHO GDWQ microbial target).
  • Reduce turbidity to <=5 NTU (WHO GDWQ aesthetic/health target) and ideally <1 NTU to enable downstream disinfection.
  • Inactivate viruses and protozoan cysts (e.g. Giardia, Cryptosporidium) - the specific gap no single current household tech closes (multi-barrier HWT: up to 3.0-log Giardia, 2.4-log Crypto, but 0-log for some without a dedicated protozoa step).
  • Produce >=15 L/person/day (UN minimum) and cover domestic + drinking + hygiene demand for the target household/setting.
  • Keep fluoride below the WHO 1.5 mg/L guideline and address arsenic/lead where present (WHO GDWQ chemical targets).
  • Meet WHO International Scheme to Evaluate HWT Technologies 3-star (or 2-star minimum) performance classification for the pathogen classes it claims to remove.
  • Be safe and acceptable: no chemical, taste/odour, or residual disinfectant byproduct hazard in the treated water (WHO GDWQ acceptability aspects, Ch.10).
  • Maintain performance across a realistic range of inlet conditions (turbidity, pH, temperature, pathogen load) without frequent expert intervention, for a defined service life (e.g. >12 months continuous).

Constraints

  • No reliable grid power at the point of use in most target settings (solar / gravity / manual only; avoid electric pumps or UV lamps as a first-order requirement).
  • Low-sun and rainy-season periods must be handled (SODIS fails on viruses in low sun) - climate-robustness across tropical, arid, and monsoon regions.
  • Locally available, low-cost, locally sourceable materials (sand, clay, fabric, metal, plastic); avoid scarce or import-dependent components.
  • Skills: buildable and operable by untrained or minimally trained household/community members; maintenance within local capacity (no specialist parts or calibrated equipment).
  • Affordability: materials + recurring consumables within a household budget (JMP/UNICEF/WHO 2021 affordability work; no common threshold yet, but must not displace other basic needs).
  • Physical carrying/transport: the treated water must be portable (jerrican-scale) where piped delivery is absent; weight and container handling constraints.
  • Connectivity: operation and monitoring must not depend on internet or smartphone; any telemetry optional.
  • Siting and water security: must work where the source is scarce (water-stressed countries >2 billion people) and resilient to drought/flood.
