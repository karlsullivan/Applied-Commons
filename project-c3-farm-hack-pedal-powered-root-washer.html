Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • The 'no-detectable generic E. coli/100 mL' criterion is a real regulatory bar: 21 CFR 112.44(a) requires that agricultural water used during/after harvest in a manner that directly contacts covered produce (including washing or cooling) have no detectable generic E. coli in 100 mL, and that untreated surface water not be used for those purposes. So this is not a loose aspiration; it is the Produce Safety Rule criterion for exactly the water a root washer uses.
  • The rule also imposes the engineering requirement that any treatment used must actually deliver the microbial criterion, be delivered consistently, and be monitored with an adequate method and frequency (21 CFR 112.46). A low-cost treatment stage therefore only counts if the washer operator tests the treated water and can show it meets no-detectable/100 mL — the design must include a cheap test-strip check, not just a filter.
  • Target settings: the Farm Hack pedal-power root washer is a once-through system — water enters from a hose, is sprayed onto the produce, and exits through the barrel stave gaps to the ground. There is no recirculation, so within one batch wash water is not re-applied to produce; the microbial quality of the wash water equals the quality of the incoming source water.
  • Baseline (context for days-to-spoilage): UC Davis - mature carrots store 7-9 months at 0C, 3-5 months at 3-5C, bunched only 10-14 days; optimum RH 98-100%; 'Free moisture from the washing process or unevaporated condensation... will promote decay.' AgriPure: damp-sand cellar storage holds crispness 5-7 months. So a washing-related rot penalty is only meaningful against a 5-9 month cold-storage baseline, never against the sub-week ambient one.
  • Chlorine sanitization is the low-cost stage that reliably reaches no-detectable for free-floating E. coli: established post-harvest guidance is to use only a food-grade, EPA-registered sanitizer, dose to the label's stated available-chlorine concentration, and verify with chlorine test strips, recording time and ppm on a log. Combined with the once-through design (fresh, treated water in; used water out), this is the most direct route to meeting 112.44(a).
  • The clearest measured washed>unwashed rot penalty at cellar temperature over full storage: UMass 2011-12 (6 varieties, 32F, stored 6 months to April) - 'washed carrots were more than twice as likely as unwashed carrots to show signs of rot (35% versus 14%), although rot damage was quite minor in all carrots.' No difference in top sprouting or Brix.
  • No measured bruise/damage rate exists for the Farm Hack pedal root washer. All five FarmHack pages I retrieved (low-cost pedalpower tool page, its wiki page, the 'root washer improvement' forum, and the motorized SARE root washer) are qualitative design/field reports giving drive, drainage and throughput (~30 lb / ~5 min batch) but no bruise or damage measurement.
  • Baseline microbial quality in the target setting is not 'clean' and is source-dependent: washing transfers surface-borne microbes (E. coli, Cyclospora, Salmonella) that live in soil and on the produce surface into the wash water, and that water can then spread them to other produce. So the wash water is a vehicle for adding pathogen load — the incoming source and the absence of recirculation are what control whether the machine adds or removes load.
