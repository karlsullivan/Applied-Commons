Environmental stability and resource security

Pollution reduction, healthy ecosystems, durable carbon storage and essential resource availability.

The need

Essentially the entire global population, with the sharpest deficits in low- and middle-income countries: ~2.1B people without safely managed drinking water and 3.4B without safely managed sanitation (2024); 1.7B using faecally contaminated drinking water; >2B living in water-stressed countries (2021), most severe in North Africa/Western Asia and South/Central Asia; 99% of the world's population in areas not meeting WHO air-quality guidelines, with 4.2M premature deaths/yr from outdoor air pollution (89% in LMICs, greatest burden in South-East Asia and Western Pacific); >3.2B people impacted by human-altered, degrading land (UNCCD), including rural communities reliant on it for food and water; and small-scale food producers, pastoralists and fishers most exposed to moderate/severe food insecurity (FAO FIES measure).

Extreme, chronic and compounding, not episodic. Mortality is continuous and large: 4.2M premature deaths/yr from outdoor air pollution, ~505,000 diarrhoeal deaths/yr from contaminated water, and a combined 6.7M premature deaths/yr when household air pollution is included. Resource losses are ongoing at scale: >35 Gt CO2/yr from fossil fuels and industry with growth that has not yet peaked; 4.3M ha of tropical primary forest lost in 2025 alone (11+ football fields per minute, still 46% higher than a decade ago); soils eroding up to 100x faster than natural replenishment; 32 Gt of land carbon projected to be lost 2015–2030. The trajectory is worsening, not stabilising: at current rates 90% of land will bear human imprint by 2050.

What people do now

Current responses are dominated by national/international policy and capital-intensive infrastructure rather than local, community-scale intervention. For air: governments act across energy, transport, industry, waste and urban planning; WHO notes most outdoor pollution sources are 'well beyond the control of individuals'. For water: the gap is capital and operations — global ODA to the water sector actually fell (US$8.9B in 2022 to US$8.7B in 2023), and large-scale safely-managed treatment serves only 56% of domestic wastewater. For carbon: emissions reduction depends on industrial decarbonisation and international finance (the unmet US$100B/yr climate-finance commitment); durable carbon storage relies on forest conservation (monitoring via open platforms like Global Forest Watch) and land-use restraint. For land/food: the UNCCD has 131 of 196 countries pledging land-degradation neutrality by 2030, but progress is slow and agriculture still degrades soil far faster than it is restored. The consistent cost signal is high capital and high skills: large plants, grid power, imported components and specialist operation — which is precisely what the affected communities most lack.

Where existing solutions fall short

Most proven solutions operate at national or industrial scale — clean power generation, large municipal water and wastewater plants, forest-protection programmes, and international finance (the long-unmet US$100B/yr climate-finance goal) — and are too capital- and skills-intensive to be built and owned by the local communities most affected. Open data and monitoring platforms (Global Forest Watch, Our World in Data) are strong and freely available but are observational only: they detect and report loss without remediating it. Capital for water, sanitation and land restoration is chronically short (water ODA is declining), and there is a pronounced absence of solutions that are simultaneously local, low-cost (tens of dollars per unit), off-grid, and independently verifiable against a published metric. Finally, current approaches treat pollution, ecosystem health, carbon storage and food security as separate problem silos, so no single locally buildable solution addresses more than one pillar at a time.

What a solution must do

  • Water: point-of-use / community treatment that reduces E. coli to <100 CFU/100 mL (a >=99.9% reduction from typical faecally contaminated source water), producing water meeting WHO drinking-water guidance, at a material cost under US$50 per household unit and operable without grid electricity.
  • Wastewater: community-scale treatment that achieves >=90% total-suspended-solids removal and >=99.9% faecal-indicator removal before discharge, and substantially reduces (halves) the local proportion of untreated wastewater reaching surface water (SDG 6.3 target).
  • Air: community- or household-scale reduction of ambient PM2.5 (and, where combustion is the source, CO) at the point of exposure, targeting a measurable step toward WHO interim target 1 (35 µg/m3 annual mean PM2.5), verifiable by a low-cost PM sensor (e.g. >=20% reduction in measured PM2.5 in the deployment area).
  • Forests / carbon: monitoring capability that detects and alerts on tropical tree-cover loss at ~30 m resolution within 14 days of the change (comparable to open GLAD/Global Forest Watch alerts), enabling response before loss is consolidated; and community restoration that achieves >=50% two-year survival of planted/established native trees while netting zero additional forest loss in the restoration area.
  • Soil / durable carbon storage: land management that reduces topsoil loss to <=50% of the pre-intervention rate and increases topsoil organic carbon by >=0.5% within a 3–5 year monitoring window, moving the local plot toward land-degradation neutrality (UNCCD LDN).
  • Food / resource availability: local intervention that measurably reduces moderate-or-severe food insecurity (FAO FIES) in the target community (e.g. a >=5 percentage-point drop in the FIES share) while increasing small-scale producer yield per unit of existing cultivated land by >=30% without net expansion of cultivated area.
  • Cross-cutting: every candidate must be buildable and maintainable by local people using locally available or locally sourceable materials, must operate on off-grid or intermittent power, and must publish a defined, independently re-checkable performance metric (the numbers above) so it can be tested against.

Constraints

  • Power: many target settings are off-grid or have intermittent, low-voltage supply; solutions must run on solar, manual, or small battery storage and tolerate brown-outs.
  • Tools: fabrication must be achievable with basic workshop tools (hand tools, a simple drill, a welder or 3D printer at best) — no specialised heavy machinery or clean-room processes.
  • Materials: components must be sourceable from local markets or fabricated from locally available inputs (plastic, metal, timber, biochar, common filters), with no long-lead imported single points of failure; spare parts locally reproducible.
  • Skills: operation and routine maintenance must be trainable by a community volunteer in 1–2 weeks; no requirement for a degree-level engineer on site.
  • Climate: must function across the local climate envelope — heavy wet/rainy seasons, high ambient temperatures, dust, and salinity where coastal.
  • Connectivity: must operate fully offline; any data (sensor, monitoring) must be stored locally and sync opportunistically, with the core function never depending on cloud access.
  • No new pollution: the intervention must not itself generate hazardous waste, require toxic reagents, or create a disposal burden the community cannot absorb (it must pass a basic life-cycle check).
