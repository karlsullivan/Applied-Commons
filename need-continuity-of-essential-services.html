Dependable services and recovery from failures, disasters and supply disruptions.

The need

Households and communities without reliable power: 730M (IEA) to 655M (WHO/Tracking SDG7) without electricity in 2024; sub-Saharan Africa = 8/10 of the global gap, rural SSA deficit grew 376M (2010) to 447M (2024); ~half the population of Haiti lacks power. Firms and industry in low- and middle-income countries exposed to unreliable power/water/transport: World Bank/GFDRR microdata of ~143,000 firms across 137 LMICs. People without safe water: 2.2B without safely managed drinking water (1.7B on a faecally contaminated source); >2B in water-stressed countries. People facing food insecurity: 673M hungry in 2024 (8.2%; 307M in Africa alone), 2.3B with moderate/severe food insecurity. Health systems and patients: a projected 11M health-worker shortfall by 2030, mostly in low- and lower-middle-income countries, concentrated in rural/remote/underserved areas. Populations in hazard-prone regions and those dependent on fragile supply chains: 4.2B people affected by major disasters 2000-2019.

Chronic, large-scale and, on several indicators, worsening or stagnating. Electricity access is nearly flat since 2020 (only -11M vs 2023 per IEA); the IEA/WHO warn the pace must roughly triple (~1.3%/yr) just to reach universal access by 2030, and sub-Saharan Africa is falling further behind as population growth outpaces electrification. Reliability harms are continuous and costly (~$300B/yr business drag from power, water and transport outages). Water and sanitation insecurity persists at ~2.2B people and is expected to worsen under climate change and population growth. Food security is backsliding where it matters most (PoU rose across most of Africa and western Asia; 512M projected undernourished by 2030, ~60% in Africa). Disaster exposure is accelerating (7,348 major events 2000-2019 vs 4,212 in 1980-1999; floods more than doubled), and every major shock interrupts essential services for days to weeks, compounding pre-existing access gaps.

What people do now

Three dominant, largely non-local patterns. (1) Utility/grid restoration and hardening: national utility agencies bring back power/water after outages and disasters; this is capital-intensive, slow (restoration of lifelines runs to days-weeks after a shock; gas systems can take months) and leaves vulnerable, electricity-dependent populations exposed during the outage. (2) On-site self-generation: because supply is unreliable, firms buy their own backup power - World Bank/GFDRR estimate self-generated electricity costs ~$64B/yr (mostly diesel generators), on top of $38B/yr in black-out utilization losses and $82B/yr in lost sales; households similarly buy kerosene, batteries and small solar home systems (much of it too small to be 'real' access). (3) Humanitarian/health recovery response: after a shock, actors reactively restore routine and essential health services per national recovery plans and WHO 'build back better' guidance, constrained by the 11M health-worker shortfall and fragile cold chain/supply lines. Global trade also relies on concentrated, fragmented shipping; a single disruption (e.g. a Suez-class event) diffuses worldwide and is tracked only post-hoc by indices such as the World Bank's Global Supply Chain Stress Index. None of these are cheap, open, or locally buildable at community scale.

Where existing solutions fall short

Access gaps are stubborn and geographically concentrated: 655-730M without power (8/10 in sub-Saharan Africa; Haiti ~half its population; rural SSA deficit grew 376M to 447M over 14 years), 2.2B without safely managed water, 673M hungry (rising across Africa and western Asia), and an 11M health-worker shortfall by 2030 - so 'continuity' is only half the problem; the service is often simply absent. Where the service exists it is unreliable and slow to recover: World Bank/GFDRR quantifies ~$300B/yr of business drag from power/water/transport outages in 137 LMICs, and the restoration literature shows lifelines take days to weeks (gas sometimes months) to return after a disaster, leaving vulnerable, electricity-dependent people exposed. The dominant current practice is the wrong shape for local action: (a) utility/grid restoration is top-down, capital-intensive and slow; (b) on-site diesel self-generation works but is expensive (~$64B/yr), polluting and fuel-dependent; (c) humanitarian/health recovery is reactive and starved of workforce and cold-chain/supply capacity; (d) global supply chains are fragmented and fragile, with disruption diffusing globally and only tracked after the fact. The unmet, locally addressable gap is cheap, open, modular and field-deployable continuity/recovery capacity - reliable backup power, water storage/disinfection, and health-service continuity (including cold chain) - that a local operator can build, run and keep running through a multi-day outage or after a disruption, with measurable uptime, cost and recovery-time targets, rather than waiting for a capital-intensive national response.

What a solution must do

  • Reliability/uptime: for the essential service it backs, meet a stated continuous-availability target - e.g. >=99% of critical loads online during scheduled outages, or restore an interrupted essential service within a defined window (e.g. <=24 h for a household/clinic-scale backup unit, faster than grid/utility restoration).
  • Cost ceiling: <= US$500 in materials for a household/clinic-scale unit and <= US$2,000 for a community-scale (100-500 household) unit, so a 1,000-household deployment costs <= US$20,000 in hardware - cheap relative to the ~$300B/yr self-generation spend it would displace.
  • Autonomy: >=7 days of operation on stored energy/standby with no mains and no external resupply (power/water/health continuity during a multi-day disaster outage).
  • Connectivity: functions with no Internet (offline, or SMS/LoRa/Wi-Fi mesh fallback); per-unit connectivity cost <= US$5/month or zero.
  • Safety/health (for any water or medical-adjacent service): deliver a defined safety bar - e.g. water stored/disinfected to a measurable pathogen reduction (>=99.9% reduction of a reference indicator organism) or maintain a stated cold-chain temperature band (2-8 C) for a defined duration.
  • Maintainability: a trained local operator (no engineer) can swap/recharge/restart the unit in <=30 minutes using a printed local-language runbook; self-diagnostic fault indication.
  • Openness: hardware, firmware and build files fully open-source with a publicly reproducible bill of materials on commodity parts; no vendor lock-in.
  • Reproducibility: buildable by a volunteer with basic tools (soldering/3D printing/assembly) in <=8 hours from an open build pack.
  • Scalability/modularity: units can be combined (N in parallel) to scale a single household to a community load, with a single consistent interface.
  • Measurement: ship with a simple, on-device log of uptime/outage duration and energy/water delivered so the operator can verify the continuity target is actually being met.

Constraints

  • Power: unreliable or absent mains; only solar/battery/standby available, with cold-weather battery derating.
  • Connectivity: low/intermittent mobile and broadband coverage; must operate offline and over cheap links.
  • Tools & skills: local builders/operators without engineering training; limited to soldering, 3D printing, basic assembly and battery handling.
  • Materials: commodity and locally sourceable or shippable in volume; must survive the environment (humidity, heat, dust, UV, flooding of the install site).
  • Climate: tropical heat and rain, flood-prone siting, dust and salt air; equipment must endure the very hazard that caused the outage.
  • Maintenance: no on-site engineers; spare parts locally obtainable; self-diagnostic; safe to service (no high-voltage or pressurized components without interlocks).
  • Regulatory/safety: for any water/medical-adjacent function, must meet a named published standard (e.g. WHO Drinking Water Quality Guidelines / a recognized disinfection standard; 2-8 C cold-chain band) rather than a vendor claim.
  • Cost ceiling: per-unit cost bounded (<=~US$500 household / <=~US$2,000 community) to stay fundable against capital-intensive utility restoration and expensive diesel self-generation.
  • Scale: single units serve a household/clinic; the solution must compose to community scale without a bespoke integration effort.
