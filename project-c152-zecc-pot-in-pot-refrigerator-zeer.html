ZECC / Pot-in-pot refrigerator (zeer)

From Applied Commons, the open catalogue of ways to meet human needs
ZECC / Pot-in-pot refrigerator (zeer)
NeedCold chain and storage
ServesFood and nutrition, Economic and livelihood security
StatusBuild pack ready
LicenceNone found
Climatesarid
SafetyControls needed
The safety review found hazards that need controls when building or using this design.

A non-electric evaporative 'zero-energy' cooler: a porous clay pot (lined with wet sand) placed inside a larger clay pot with a wet cloth on top; evaporation of the interstitial water draws heat from the food. It is an open, public-domain, local-materials design invented by Mohamed Bah Abba in rural northern Nigeria (mass-produced at ~US$1/pair, disseminated by NGOs Practical Action, Humanity First, and MIT D-Lab, with open build guides from Movement e.V.). It directly fills the brief's zero-electricity / dry-climate tier: locally buildable from local materials, ~US$1, no grid, no refrigerant, locally maintainable. It is explicitly wet-bulb-limited: effective only in hot, dry (roughly <40% RH) climates and for non-climacteric, high-humidity-tolerant produce -- not a humid-equatorial solution and not a cold-climate solution.

Assembly

Tools: Inner pot and a larger outer vessel leaving a 3-5 cm gap (outer must be clay; inner may be clay, plastic or metal), plus a clay lid, Clean sand (several litres per unit), Clean water and a watering can or basin, Sieve or 3-5 mm wire mesh, or a thin cloth to sieve dust through, Wet cloth / burlap (jute) to cover the opening, A stool and a ground cloth for winnowing, A stand to raise the assembled unit ~30 cm for under-airflow, Flat stone, water and a scraper/knife (only if making the pots), If making pots: clay + donkey dung (~3:2) or a local workable clay body, clay or saucepan moulds, optional potter's wheel, kiln fuel (wood + cow dung) and rocks for the kiln ring, Optional thermometer to verify internal temperature
Skills: Pottery / hand-building (or sourcing) of clay vessels, if the pots are not bought, Careful, steady handling of fragile, dry-fired clay, Sieving and winnowing sand to remove stones and fine dust, Evenly packing sand around the inner pot while keeping it centred, Watering to saturation without over-saturating (watching for the floating inner pot), Siting the unit for shade and airflow
Time: ~30-60 minutes per unit to assemble once the pots and clean sand are in hand (plus ~15 minutes to sieve/winnow the sand); if the pots must be made locally, add several hours of forming and 2 days (summer) to 4 days (winter) of sun-drying plus a 24-hour kiln firing before assembly is possible.

  1. Choose the location and plan to assemble in place Pick a cool, shaded, well-ventilated spot exposed to outside breeze, out of direct sunlight. The assembled cooler is heavy and hard to move, so assemble it where it will actually be used. Elevate it on a stand (~30 cm high) so air can flow underneath - a raised, open position cools better than floor level or an enclosed space.
    Caution: Work on a stable surface; keep the unit out of direct sun and out of poorly ventilated or enclosed spaces where the sand cannot evaporate and the unit overheats.
  2. Obtain (or locally make) the two clay pots You need an inner pot and a larger outer vessel that leave a 3-5 cm gap for sand. The assembly guide accepts three layouts: pot-in-pot (inner may be clay, plastic or metal; outer must be clay), clay pot in a plastic/metal basin, or clay pot in a clay dish. If you must make the pots yourself (the assembly guide assumes you buy them), the Practical Action manual gives: form a mould (dig a small hole, mat plus wood-chip lining, press a stone to a bowl shape, dry 30 min), press a ~10 mm clay disc over the inverted mould and smooth it with a wet flat stone, build up the walls by hand, add a thick rim (small pot ~20 mm, large pot ~30 mm), sun-dry (2 days summer / 4 days winter), then fire in a rock-ring kiln of wood plus cow dung for 24 h. Reference mould dimensions in that manual: 370/470 mm (large) and 330/250 mm (small); the clay body is mixed roughly 3 parts soil : 2 parts dung (or a local workable clay).
    Caution: The firing is an open 24-hour wood fire - a real fire hazard; clear the ground, build the ring of rocks, supervise to burnout, and keep the green (wet) pots well away from the fire until fully dried. Do not handle pots before they are fully dried and fired.
  3. Check the pots Inspect both pots for cracks, chips or sharp rims before use. The inner pot should be food-safe; the guide notes a glazed or cement-lined inner wall lets you drive the cooling with undrinkable water such as seawater without contaminating the food, and the Gambia brief recommends keeping the inner pot's porosity as low as possible so water does not soak in during cool nights.
    Caution: Discard any cracked or chipped pot - a broken rim can cut hands and a cracked body will not hold the sand structure or the produce.
  4. Prepare the sand - remove stones and coarse particles Sieve the sand to remove stones and large particles, either by hand or through a 3-5 mm wire mesh. Removing stones stops them taking up space in the gap and forming uneven water pockets.
    Caution: Wear eye protection if sieving by hand in dusty conditions; keep the wind in your face (or winnow upwind) so the dust does not blow back onto you.
  5. Prepare the sand - remove the dust Remove as much fine dust as possible so it will not clog the pores of the clay outer pot, since clogged pores stop evaporation. The guide gives two methods: sieve the sand through a thin cloth, or 'winnow' - slowly pour the sand from shoulder height (use a stool if needed) onto a cloth laid on the ground, letting the wind carry the dust away. Repeat until the sand is clean.
    Caution: Winnowing throws a cloud of dust - do it with your back to the prevailing wind or wear a mask, and hold the cloth in place so the clean sand does not blow off.
  6. Add the base sand layer Pour a layer of sand into the bottom of the outer pot to make an even, level surface for the inner pot to sit on. The sand may be wet or dry at this stage.
    Caution: Keep the base layer even and not lumpy, or the inner pot will sit at an angle and the gap will be uneven.
  7. Insert and centre the inner pot Gently lower the inner pot onto the sand bed and centre it. Make sure the top of the inner pot is at least 2 cm higher than the rim of the outer pot (the assembly guide's figure); the Practical Action manual's alternate assembly instead has the two tops level. Keep a 3-5 cm gap around the inner pot.
    Caution: Lower the inner pot gently and keep it centred - a tilted pot cracks the outer pot's wall and leaves an uneven sand gap.
  8. Fill the gap with sand Pack the space between the two pots with the prepared sand, working it in evenly all the way around until the top is flush. Keep the 3-5 cm gap so the sand can hold and wick the water; do not force or shift the inner pot.
    Caution: Pack by hand with gentle, even pressure; avoid dropping sand or striking the pots, which can crack the dry-fired clay.
  9. Water the sand to saturation Add enough clean water to thoroughly wet the sand between the pots. Enough has been added when the water takes a few seconds to absorb from the top. If the inner pot begins to float, too much water has been added - drain the excess before using the cooler. In field trials the unit was watered about twice a day with roughly 0.5-3 L per watering (a small unit used ~1 L twice daily).
    Caution: Drain overflow if the inner pot floats; standing water around the base can soak the inner pot and waterlog or contaminate the produce. Use clean water.
  10. Fill the inner pot with produce Store only fruits and vegetables that are dry, in good condition, and free of dirt and insects, and store compatible items together: ethylene-producers (melon, avocado, banana, mango, tomato) spoil ethylene-sensitive produce, so keep them separate from vegetables and from each other, and keep strong-odour items (garlic, onion) apart. Do NOT store meat, dairy, medicines or vaccines, and do not store items that rot in high humidity (potatoes, carrots, sweet peppers, eggplant, cabbage) or grains (cereals/millet) - the device is for fruit and vegetable only.
    Caution: Follow the Gambia brief's hygiene rules: do not touch the stored produce with bare hands, and disinfect the inner pot after it is emptied before restocking.
  11. Cover the opening Wet a cloth or piece of burlap (jute), squeeze out the excess water, and place it over the top of the inner pot so the opening is completely covered; fold a damp burlap several times and lay it over the cooler. The Gambia field procedure then placed a clay lid over the mouth of the jar as well.
    Caution: Squeeze out excess water before placing the cloth so it does not drip onto or soak the produce, and never leave the cooler uncovered.
  12. Verify it is cooling (recommended) Place a thermometer in the inner pot (and one in the outside air) to confirm the unit is cooling. Field data: the Gambia trial recorded an average ~10.5 C lower internal than external temperature (32.6 C outside vs 22.2 C inside), and the 2009 Sudan study showed an elevated unit in shade out-performed one in direct sun by ~3 C. If it is not cooling, check that it sits in a dry, ventilated spot, the sand is damp, and local humidity is low enough - the device works in dry climates (roughly below ~40 % relative humidity).
    Caution: Do not use the unit to store vaccines or medicines - it cannot hold 2-8 C and is explicitly not suitable for the medical cold chain.
  13. Operate and maintain daily Keep the sand and the covering cloth damp at all times - typically re-water once a day (more in hot, windy, low-humidity conditions, less when humid). Keep the unit in shade, out of direct sunlight and in good air circulation, and sponge the exterior clean to keep dust off. Do not leave it uncovered, do not let the sand or cloth dry out, and do not store it in a poorly ventilated or enclosed space.
    Caution: Check daily so the sand does not dry out (which stops cooling and can crack the pot) and so the unit stays shaded and ventilated.
  14. Clean down and restock When the produce is consumed or removed, empty the inner pot, disinfect it, and let it dry before putting in a new stock.
    Caution: Wash hands before and after handling produce and disinfect the inner pot to prevent cross-contamination between batches.

Testing

CheckHowPass if
Site suitability (device is only certified for a location where it can work)Before/alongside testing, measure ambient relative humidity and max daily temperature at the site (hygrometer + thermometer or weather data for the dry season). Check shade and natural airflow at the intended position, and confirm water can be added 1-3 times per day.RH < ~40% and max daily temperature > ~25 C in shade with airflow; if not met, the unit is out of its operating envelope (wet-bulb limited) and must not be certified for that site. (Operating conditions per D-Lab Best Practices Guide / CoolVeg guide.)
Assembly integrity / leak testVisual + water test: inspect both pots for cracks, glaze chips and firing defects; verify the inner pot's food-contact surface is glazed or cemented (impermeable) if undrinkable water will be used; fill the annulus with clean fine sand and water to the damp (not waterlogged) state; place the unit level on a flat, solid base with a catch basin; verify the wet cloth + lid seals the top.No standing water escape after a full soak and 24 h; no water reaching the inner food space (inner pot stays dry); sand feels damp, never pooling; unit stable, cannot tip. (Crack/leak checks from the Practical Action Gambia report, the Desert Fridge build guide, and the zeerpot.com maintenance guide.)
Temperature depression (cooling works)Dual-thermometer / data-logger protocol (the Practical Action Gambia field method): one calibrated thermometer inside the inner pot among the produce, one outside in the same shaded position; read both at the same time daily (midday, ~13:00, when depression is lowest) for >= 3 consecutive days with the unit at operating condition (sand re-wet 1-3x/day, covered). D-Lab lab trials used Sensen data-loggers for continuous T+RH of test units vs an ambient control.Internal temperature at least 5-8 C below ambient over the test period (sustained field performance); 10 C or more is documented in dry-climate field runs (Gambia trial: 10.47 C average at 32.6 C ambient; DIY literature: 5-15 C drop; 'at least 8 C below max daily ambient'). A unit that cannot beat ambient by a several-degree margin fails.
Shelf-life extension vs ambient controlSame produce, same harvest, two batches: one in the certified unit, one in an identical container in the ambient location (the Gambia trial's control was a plain unglazed clay pot in the open). Score daily with the D-Lab lab panel - visual inspection, wilting index, colour (colorimetry), firmness, weight loss; for tomatoes also acidity / vitamin C in a lab setting. Record the day each batch becomes 'fit for human consumption / sale'.At least ~2-3x the ambient batch's fresh window. Benchmarks from field/lab: Gambia tomatoes - control deteriorated day 5 (fully spoiled), zeer tomatoes fit for consumption to day 14 (~3x); leafy greens 1-2 days -> 4+ days (D-Lab lab); tomatoes 2 -> 20 days and carrots 4 -> 20 days in the Practical Action Sudan deployment (2.5x to ~10x range).
Water use (operating cost sanity)Log all water added during the temperature test (the Gambia protocol logged first/second watering times and litres per day).Order of magnitude 1.5-2.5 L/day for a standard pair (Engineering for Change field figure, secondhand - treat as an order-of-magnitude bound, not a spec). Consistent with 1-3 waterings/day in a dry climate.
Hygiene / food-safety state at handoverCheck food-contact surfaces (inner pot, cloth, lid) are clean and undamaged; verify the operating instructions state: only clean, infection-free produce; no bare-hand handling of stored contents; disinfect the unit between batches; cover the top; keep the unit sheltered from rain and out of direct sun.Pass: all four hygiene rules stated in the unit's usage sheet and demonstrated by the operator. (Practical Action Gambia recommendations; zeerpot.com hygiene guidance.)

Failure modes

FailureEffectMitigation
High ambient humidity (wet season / humid equatorial climate)Cooling collapses: at ~30 C the pot-in-pot reaches only ~0.5 C below ambient at 80% RH vs ~8 C at 40% RH; 'when the air is totally saturated, no cooling occurs'. Adoption decay in Mali tracked the rainy season.Certify only for dry-climate sites (RH < ~40%); pause/relocate use through the wet season (the Mali programme paused dissemination until after the rainy season); for humid sites, a different enabler (mechanical cooling) is required - this device is the wrong tool.
Sand dries out (missed watering)Evaporation stops; the inner pot reverts to ambient and the produce spoils on the accelerated timeline; the failure is silent - nothing looks wrong until the produce rots.Twice-daily (up to 3x/day in hot dry weather) moisture check with water top-up; a water reservoir (inverted airtight jar, shallow pool, or wicking supply) extends autonomy; monitor internal temperature daily - the temperature climb is the early warning.
Sand waterlogged / over-watered (rain or excess top-up)Standing water slows evaporation and reduces cooling; water can pool at the base, rot the structure and create slipping/algae; the Gambia trial noted water observed in the inner jar.Keep sand damp, not saturated; shelter from rain; provide drainage or a catch basin; reduce daily top-up until the sand dries slightly; replace waterlogged sand.
Cracked, un-fired, or low-porosity (glazed) outer potLeaks waste water and can flood the base; a dense, un-fired (or over-fired, low-porosity) clay wall evaporates poorly so cooling is weak; a cracked inner pot contaminates food with the interstitial water.Reject units with visible cracks before soak test; fire pots fully (the Appropedia build: 24 h firing under dung/sticks); choose earthenware (porous) for the outer pot and glazed/cemented inner pot; repair or replace rather than field-patch.
Enclosed, still-air placement (indoor / under a table / in a corner)Stagnant humid air around the pot kills the evaporation gradient; the Gambia field users kept units in enclosed spaces and 'were not consistently getting the temperature inside the cooler as low as the experimental temperatures'.Install in a shaded but well-ventilated position with natural airflow; raise on a stand; open area or a position exposed to breeze; if indoors, only near a ventilated opening.
Direct, prolonged sun on the unitHeats the outer pot and sand, raises the baseline, and can crack unglazed clay; can overdrive daytime evaporation so the sand is dry by evening.Partial shade (under an awning or near a wall) as standard placement guidance; extra shading in extreme heat.
Mould / algae / microbial growth in the unit (worn, damp, uncleaned)Contaminates food-contact surfaces; the Gambia trial observed fungal growth on the thermometer support and two tomatoes introduced by hands; worn porous clay can 'harbour germs'.Clean pots every few weeks (brush, rinse, dry fully); remove and clean any mould with water-vinegar; disinfect between batches; keep the unit ventilated and rain-protected; replace the sand if it clumps or smells.
Storing wrong products (grains, onions, garlic, meat, dairy, medicine, ethylene producers mixed with ethylene-sensitive produce)Grains and low-humidity crops gain moisture in the cool humid atmosphere (evaporative devices are explicitly unsuitable for them); meat/dairy/medicine are unsafe because the unit never holds below 20 C; ethylene-releasing produce accelerates ripening of neighbours and reduces quality.Restrict the unit to humidity-tolerant fruit/vegetables (tomatoes, leafy greens, peppers, okra, eggplant, carrots, cucumbers, zucchini); separate ethylene producers; route grains to hermetic (PICS-type) storage and medicines/meat/dairy to mechanical refrigeration.
Capacity mismatch / overloadA single pot pair is household-scale (about 12 kg of vegetables in field units); Mali users whose stated needs were 46-196 kg cited the small capacity as a reason not to adopt; overloading the inner pot blocks air circulation and drops performance.Right-size to the user's real holding volume before build (survey first - the brief's utilisation rule); do not overpack the inner pot; for market-scale needs use a ZECC brick chamber (500-5,000 L) or a forced-air chamber, not more pots.
Frequent opening of the unitWarm dry air enters each time the cover is lifted, eroding the cool humid headspace and the shelf-life gain.Open as infrequently as possible (Practical Action Gambia recommendation; the ZECC equivalent is the same rule); plan batch retrieval.

Seasonal temperature floor (cold nights / cool winter)Evaporation slows in cool, humid weather; the cooling margin shrinks toward zero and stored produce can be no colder than ambient - the device is a hot-season technology.Reduce watering in cool weather (do not let the sand dry fully but do not saturate); treat the unit as seasonal in any climate with cool months; note in the certification that it is a dry-season device.

Safety

Passive evaporative device - no electrical/battery/gas/pressure/radio hazards. Hygiene, product-restriction, water-barrier, tipping and breakage hazards are already covered by the pack's acceptance criteria and failure modes. Add controls for: lead/cadmium glaze verification of the inner pot (food-contact ceramic standard), silica-dust control during sand preparation, fire/CO safety for the optional open-fire pot firing, and PPE for wet cement lining.

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • A single passive clay-pot (zeer) cooler is capped at ~150 L inner-pot storage; the guide states 'if the vegetables can fit into a clay pot with a capacity of 150 liters or less, then a clay pot cooler is most appropriate.' Typical field units store ~12 kg of vegetables for ~20 days.
  • The passive brick 'zero energy cool chamber' (ZECC) - the documented scaled-up form of the same technology - is designed for ~500 to 5,000 L storage (roughly 350-700 kg of produce), intended for farmers, groups, and cooperatives. It still does not reach 2 t per single unit.
  • A scaled forced-air evaporative cooling chamber DOES reach ~2 t at ~$10,000: the CoolVeg/MIT D-Lab 20-ft shipping-container chamber holds 168 crates (>3,000 kg of produce) and is priced at $10,500 (on-grid) / $15,000 (off-grid, incl. solar+battery), or ~$3.13/$4.46 per kg of capacity. Portable versions (15-30 crates, ~500 kg) cost $900-$2,300.
  • The ~$10,000 / ~2 t configuration is a fan-driven, solar-powered forced-air evaporative system, NOT a passive clay-pot ZECC. It uses an industrial exhaust fan pushing air through a wet pad to rapidly pre-cool produce (e.g., 500 kg by 10 C in <6 h on <200 W). It is the same underlying evaporative principle and the same D-Lab lineage, but a different, powered device. The passive pot-in-pot cannot reach the brief's target; only this forced-air derivative can.
  • The zeer (pot-in-pot) is a non-electric evaporative cooler whose cooling is bounded by the wet-bulb; it performs poorly or not at all at high ambient humidity and is documented as most effective in low-humidity climates (<40% RH) with max daily temperature >25 degC and 1-3 waterings/day in a dry ventilated space.
  • A humidity-independent sealed/dry-store variant for grain already exists and is the field-standard answer: hermetic (oxygen-limited) dry storage - the PICS triple bag (2 HDPE liners + woven polypropylene), the Grain-pro bag, or sealed steel drums. Once the bag/drum is sealed, ambient humidity is excluded and grain is stored by O2 depletion/dehydration of pests, so performance does not depend on wet- vs dry-season RH.
  • Performance context: passive ZECC/zeer units lower temperature ~8-18 C below ambient at ~90-95% RH in hot, dry climates (RH < 40%, daily max > 25 C), extending vegetable shelf life 5-10x (e.g., tomatoes 2 -> 20 days). They are not suitable for items needing sustained < 20 C (meat, dairy, medicine) or low-humidity storage (onions, grains). The forced-air chamber is additionally limited to high-humidity-tolerant produce.
  • Cost comparison at scale: the 20-ft container forced-air chamber ($10,500 on-grid) is ~60% cheaper than a comparable 3-tonne refrigerated cold room (>$52,000 off-grid) and far cheaper per kg of capacity ($3.13 vs ~$17.33 $/kg). So if the brief's real need is ~2 t market aggregation at ~$10,000, forced-air evaporative cooling is the documented, cheaper path - passive ZECC is the wrong design for that target.
