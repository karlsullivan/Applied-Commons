LBNL / Boston Children's Hospital / PIH low-cost re-usable electricity-free (passive PCM) infant warmer

From Applied Commons, the open catalogue of ways to meet human needs
LBNL / Boston Children's Hospital / PIH low-cost re-usable electricity-free (passive PCM) infant warmer
NeedReproductive and early-life needs
StatusBuild pack ready
LicenceNone found
Climatestropical, arid, temperate, cold
SafetyNeeds a qualified person
The safety review found that parts of this build need a qualified person.

Inexpensive, re-usable, non-electric infant warmer documented in an open-access paper, used to complement kangaroo mother care (KMC) or as the sole external heat source. Fully offline/passive (no battery or mains). In a prospective study in rural Rwandan hospitals (33 infants, 102 encounters, up to 6 h), it corrected hypothermia in 95% of hypothermic encounters (41/43) and prevented hypothermia in 100% of at-risk encounters (59/59), maintaining goal temperature for the study duration in >=85% of uses. Directly fits the power-free neonatal thermal-care requirement; being passive it works year-round in cold climates where electronic warmers are scarce or fail.

Assembly

Tools: Source of boiling water: stove or fire plus a kettle (the device is charged with 'the amount of water typically found in a kettle', per LBNL IPO 2015-177), Sturdy, wide-base insulated flask/thermos with removable lid, sized to receive the rolled mattress (IPO 2015-177 describes a customized insulated flask that 'keeps the water hot and protects users from burns'), The unit kit itself: PCM mattress (double-sleeved sealed film with embedded ~10 g differential-melting indicator), insulating sleeve through which the indicator stays visible, pictorial-based instructions, Flat, stable work surface for the flask and mat; hat for the infant (use encouraged in the field protocol), Standard hospital cleanser for washing (no dedicated tooling; the film construction deliberately avoids fabric/Velcro to stay washable)
Skills: No specialist skills; nurses, midwives, caregivers and (with supervision) family use after short training - documented training was a 2-hour session per health center (2019 pilot, PMC6820166), and every preparation/use/cleaning observed was correct across the 102 pilot encounters (2018 pilot, PMC6361488) and 892 RCT uses (PMC8102718), Safe handling of boiling water (kettle and flask burn risk), Reading the pictorial, low-literacy instructions printed on the device (IPO 2015-177), Following the infant temperature monitoring schedule and stop criteria (clinical, not mechanical)
Time: One-time kit receiving and inspection: ~10-15 minutes per unit. One on-demand charge: ~30 minutes in boiling water per the documented protocol (the 2018 pilot's protocol called for a 30-min immersion; the 2019 pilot found nurses wanted the preparation time shortened), plus cool-down to a safe handling temperature (no cool-down duration is published upstream). With a pre-melted standby pad, a ready unit is available in minutes - the documented standby-rotation practice: 85% of providers in the 2025 Mexico deployment kept the warmer prepared in advance, and preparation time was the most-cited drawback (15% of providers), which is why advance preparation is the documented mitigation (JOGH 15:04180; PMC6820166).

  1. 1. Receive and inspect the unit kit (one time per unit) Confirm the documented kit contents: the wax mattress (sealed double-sleeved film, 45.7 x 25.4 x 1.91 cm, ~1.2 kg of ~38C wax in 12 x ~100 g candle enclosures, built-in indicator enclosures), the insulating sleeve, and pictorial-based low-literacy instructions printed on the device (US20210069045A1; LBNL IPO 2015-177; the RCT stand-alone-use figure shows the mat in its insulating sleeve, PMC8102718). Inspect the film for punctures, pinholes or any wax weeping at the seams; check the indicator enclosures are present and the printed pictorial ink is legible (fading instruction ink was one of the documented wear modes, PMC6820166). Quarantine any unit with a compromised seal - wax leaking outside the plastic film was the failure mode seen in 5 warmers during the 892-use RCT (PMC8102718).
    Caution: Never use a unit with visible wax leakage or an open film; do not puncture, cut or open the film to 'check' it - the wax is the product and the seal is the safety boundary.
  2. 2. Set up the charging station Place the wide-base insulated flask on a stable surface near a stove or fire and a reliable water source; have the kettle and a clean work area ready. Keep at least one pre-melted standby pad available so a ready warmer exists in minutes when a hypothermic infant is identified - this pre-melt rotation is the documented mitigation for the ~30 min melt delay: the 2019 Rwandan study found nurses concerned that preparation took too long and the warmer was not readily available for urgent cases, so the 2025 Mexico deployment built advance preparation explicitly into its teaching material (PMC6820166; JOGH 15:04180).
    Caution: Boiling water is the only energy input: keep the kettle and flask out of reach of children, pour carefully, and do not overfill the flask. The device operates without electricity but does require water; in the 2025 Mexico deployment 'the need to use water' was explicitly cited as a drawback by providers, so a water source plan belongs with the station (JOGH 15:04180).
  3. 3. Melt (charge) the mattress Fill the flask with freshly boiled water (a kettle's worth, per IPO 2015-177), remove the lid, roll the mattress into a tight cylinder, and insert it into the flask; leave it for approximately 30 minutes until the wax is fully melted. The mat is designed for this roll-up-into-the-flask charging geometry (US20210069045A1). If a standby pad is already charged, skip to step 6 with it.
    Caution: Handle the flask with care during and after the melt; the contents are at or near boiling temperature. Do not add the mat before the water is boiled - the protocol specifies melting by boiled water (PMC6820166; LBNL news 2021).
  4. 4. Pass the indicator readiness gate and cool Verify the built-in indicator before any infant contact. The indicator is a separate differential-melting-point PCM (~10 g per enclosure, PureTemp44) that changes from liquid to solid at 40-45C (43C in the disclosed embodiment), i.e. above the ~38C working wax (PureTemp37). The logic is: while the indicator is still liquid/transparent the mat is TOO HOT to use; the moment the indicator has solidified, the mat is safe for use even though the main wax is still liquid and still warm (US20210069045A1, FIGS 1-2). Remove the mat from the flask, replace the lid, and let the film cool to a safe handling temperature before it contacts an infant. Upstream says only 'melted using boiling water and allowed to cool prior to use' (Berkeley Lab News Center, 2021) - no exact cool-down time or temperature threshold is published, so the practical gate is: indicator solid AND film at a handling temperature safe for the infant (this cool-down duration is not published in any upstream source and is filled from field practice; see gaps).
    Caution: Never place an infant on a mat whose film is still at or near boiling temperature; the whole point of the gate is to prevent a burn the device's own safety record - no adverse events across the 1,074 documented uses, J Glob Health 2025 - depends on this cooling step.
  5. 5. Insert the mat into the insulating sleeve Slip the cooled mat into the insulating sleeve (the documented device configuration: the mat rests in an insulating sleeve that keeps the built-in indicator visible so staff can check the temperature without opening it, PMC6820166; US20210069045A1). The sleeve is what holds the mat at goal temperature (~37C) for ~6 h once charging is done (PMC6820166; Berkeley Lab news 2021).
    Caution: Check the built-in indicator (visible through the sleeve) before the mat meets the infant; the sleeve must not be omitted - it is the thermal regulation layer between the 37C mat and the baby.
  6. 6. Place the infant and begin warming Lay the infant directly on the warmer (stand-alone heat source) or place the warmer over the infant's back while the mother provides KMC, per the documented dual use modes (RCT: 'the heat was provided by placing the warmer over the infant's back while the mother provided KMC'; standalone mode: infant laid directly on the warmer in its insulating sleeve, PMC8102718 Fig. 3-4). Swaddle the infant and the warmer together in a blanket; a hat is encouraged; avoid extra clothing on the infant as it reduces heat transfer (PMC6820166; clothing reduced the odds of successful warming in the RCT, p=0.001). In the RCT the mean duration of use was 5.5 h (SD 2.9 h) (PMC8102718).
    Caution: Start monitoring immediately: infant temperature every 15 minutes for the first hour, then hourly (documented pilot protocol, PMC6820166).
  7. 7. Monitor to the stop criteria Goal is euthermia: hypothermia defined as <36C and euthermia 36.5-37.5C in the Rwandan protocol used by the pilots (PMC6820166). End-of-use conditions, as documented: 6 hours elapsed, or the mat temperature falls below 35C as shown by the indicator, or the mother requests to stop (typically once the infant is euthermic and KMC resumes) (PMC6820166). RCT field data: the mat stayed >35C for at least 6 h in 72% of uses (PMC8102718). If the infant is hypothermic and the temperature is not rising at >=0.5C/h (or has not begun rising within 30 min), or falls below 36C despite the warmer, remove the infant to an electric heat source if one is available - the documented stop criteria (PMC6820166).
    Caution: Watch for hyperthermia (>37.5C): mild hyperthermia occurred in ~3-10% of documented uses, with lower maximum temperatures in warmer users than non-users in the RCT (38.2 vs 40.1C) - it can happen with or without the device, so the infant's own temperature, not the mat's, is the decision variable (PMC6820166; PMC8102718).
  8. 8. Maintain the standby rotation for continuous coverage One charge lasts ~6 h, but the need window can be 8-24 h of skin-to-skin coverage: before the current pad drops below 35C, start melting the next pad (step 3) so a fresh charge is ready when the first ends. This is the documented standby-rotation practice (85% of providers pre-melted in the 2025 Mexico deployment; the 2019 Rwandan study - cited as reference 19 in that Mexico paper - found nurses concerned that preparation time could delay urgent use, so deployment training explicitly emphasized preparing the warmer in advance of need) (JOGH 15:04180; PMC6820166).
    Caution: Never swap a still-hot mat directly under an infant - every pad passes the step-4 gate before contact, including standby pads taken straight from the flask.
  9. 9. Clean and re-stage the unit After use, wipe down the mattress and sleeve with standard cleanser (documented practice: in the 2018 pilot, 'The warmer was washed after each use with standard hospital cleansers', PMC6361488; the patent states it is 'designed to be easily washable with standard hospital cleansers', US20210069045A1). The film construction (no fasteners, no fabric) is what keeps it washable and re-usable over many cycles (US20210069045A1: sleeves double-sleeved 'to ensure no leakage of PCM over many repeated cycles of heating and cooling'). Dry, re-check the seal and indicator, and return the mat to the charging station for the next melt cycle (step 3) - re-running the full melt is the normal re-charge; the wax is rated for consistent performance over thousands of melt/solidify cycles (PureTemp product data, puretemp.com/products).
    Caution: After each wash, inspect the film again before the next charge; a cracked or peeled film is a retirement trigger, not a clean-and-continue case (wax leakage and sleeve peeling were the observed wear modes, PMC6820166; PMC8102718).
  10. 10. Retire the unit when wear criteria are met Retire (do not field-repair) when any of the documented wear modes appears: wax leaking outside the plastic film (5 units in the RCT), internal leak between film layers (a prototype developed this after 23 uses), indicator dropping to the cold zone too early (another prototype failed this at 32 uses), fading of the low-literacy instruction ink, or sleeve peeling - 5/12 prototypes (42%) showed some wear in the 2019 health-center pilot and 7/12 (58%) when the two Rwandan pilots are pooled, which is why production moved to stronger plastic, an improved radiofrequency sealing technique, and improved indicator and ink (PMC8102718; PMC6820166; Berkeley Lab news 2021). The design target lifetime is 1000 uses at a cost well under US$100 per unit (PMC6820166).
    Caution: A retired unit with a broken seal must not be re-entered into service by local 'resealing' - no field re-sealing procedure exists in any upstream source.

Known gaps

What the original design does not document:

  • No public per-component unit price; PureTemp PCM sold by inquiry/quote, and the LBNL IPO licensing page (ipo.lbl.gov) is JS/Cloudflare-walled so license terms and unit cost are not retrievable -> all unit_cost and total_cost left null
  • Per-product PureTemp 37 / PureTemp 44 datasheets (exact transition temperature, latent heat J/g, cycle-life number) are not published on the retrieved catalog page (catalog is general; 'Data Sheets' section not fetched)
  • Film gauge/thickness, RF-seal process parameters, and exact indicator-enclosure geometry/dimensions are not published in any retrieved source
  • Manufacturing tooling (stamping dies for the PE/nylon film) and assembly/quality process are not documented
  • Blanket material and size are not documented (kit mention only)
  • No open-source BOM file exists for this project; the BOM is reconstructed from the issued patent embodiment plus clinical/implementation papers (the warmer is a licensed, manufactured product, not assembled from field-available common materials)
  • No build-from-parts procedure exists upstream: the PCM mattress, double-sleeved film seal, and indicator are manufactured by a licensed supplier; there are no published fabrication steps, quantities, or drawings beyond the design patent (USD773681S1) and patent figures, so local construction from field-available materials is not documented.
  • Cool-down duration/temperature threshold after the 30-min melt is not quantified in any upstream source ('allowed to cool prior to use' is all that is stated); step 4's safe-handling gate is filled from field practice, cited accordingly.
  • Itemized kit contents (exact flask model, hat, spare sleeve quantities, per-item cost breakdown) are not published; only the design target (unit cost well under US$100, lifetime 1000 uses) is documented.
  • Maintenance/inspection schedule: upstream documents per-use checks (pre-use inspection, post-wash inspection) but no calendar- or cycle-count-based interval; the 1000-use lifetime is a design goal, not yet field-proven in any retrieved source.
  • Cleaning specifics (type/dilution of cleanser, air-dry vs contact-dry time) are not specified beyond 'standard hospital cleansers'.
  • The 2018 first pilot used 12 hand-made prototypes (2/12 broke down within 10 uses); production-unit manufacturing tolerances and QC criteria for the seal are not published in the retrieved sources.
  • No published, named laboratory/bench test standard is cited for a finished unit: the papers state the design meets 'all major qualifications put forward by UNICEF and PATH' but do not cite a specific published test standard with numeric acceptance limits.
  • No numeric per-unit cost is given in the retrieved sources (described only as 'inexpensive'/'low-cost'); the ~US$40/unit and <US$100 figures are design/need-brief targets, not measured values in these pages.
  • Long-term durability is a target (1000 cycles) still in testing; the 2018 breakdowns (<10 uses) occurred on hand-made prototypes, so no validated long-cycle field durability data is documented.
  • No dedicated high-temperature (37-40 C) / high-humidity storage stability test is reported; the heat-stable, no-cold-chain claim rests on the passive-PCM design rather than a retrieved storage test.
  • No microbiological/sterility test protocol quantifies cleaning and reusability efficacy across repeated washes (cleaning is described qualitatively as wiping/washing).
  • No standardized thermal bench procedure with tolerances/uncertainty is given; the '>=6 h at 37 C' figure comes from the design description, not a published thermal test method.
  • Validation is limited to consortium sites (Rwanda, Malawi, Mexico); no independent multi-site replication outside the LBNL/GNS/PIH/BCH collaboration is documented.

Safety

Passive wax-PCM infant warmer: no electrical, battery, gas-combustion, pressure, mechanical, radio, or structural hazard. Residual hazards are heat (boiling-water charging scald and infant-contact burn, gated by the 40-45 C indicator + cool-down), medical use on neonates (strong multi-trial safety record, but FDA 'product-not-device' with no quantified reprocessing test, so keep under trained staff), and low-severity chemical (non-toxic biobased wax on seal failure). The device is a manufactured, licensed product; the pack is an operating procedure, not a from-scratch build. Safe to build/deploy with the listed controls, provided placement on infants and stop-criteria are run by trained medical/midwifery staff.

Evidence

The strongest findings from Applied Commons research on this project:

  • Melt time is ~30 minutes: 'The mattress is rolled up and placed in an accompanying sturdy, wide-based thermos filled with boiling water, and the PCM is allowed to melt, which happens in approximately 30 min.' Stated independently in both Nahimana et al 2018 and May et al 2019; boiling can be done by coal (no electricity) or kettle/oven.
  • Validated supply chain: manufactured by Global Newborn Solutions (GNS nonprofit, US), under LBNL/UC Regents patent license (Infant Warming Pad; 'Differential-Melting Point PCM as safety indicator' US patent app, Aug 2020). US FDA determined it a 'product' not a 'device' (outside FDA device certification); cleared by TUV; approved Class A by Rwanda FDA; approved as a thermoregulatory option by Malawi Ministry of Health, Aug 2022, with MoH planning nationwide rollout.
  • Wear in the health-center cohort (Phase II pilot, 12 warmers, 102 uses): 'Five out of 12 warmers (42%) showed signs of wear and tear'; combined across both pilot phases 'A total of 7/12 (58%) warmers demonstrated wear and tear'. (This confirms the question's '5/12 pads' figure.)
  • Component wears first (Phase II pilot, 12 warmers): the internal plastic/PCM seal failed first - one warmer developed an internal leak after 23 uses (water moving between the inside plastic layers) - and the temperature indicator failed early next (one fell into the cold zone too early after 32 uses). Later-occurring wear: 3 warmers' low-literacy instruction ink faded, and one insulating sleeve began peeling. So the plastic seal and the temperature indicator are the wear-first components, ahead of ink and sleeve.
  • Cool-down is up to 15 minutes: after removing the melted mattress, its temperature is checked against a liquid-crystal color indicator marking the safe/effective zone (38 C to 35 C); 'If the mattress remains at >38 C, it is allowed to cool until it falls into the safe zone... which can take up to 15 min.' Worst-case solid-to-ready is therefore ~45 minutes; the melt+cool-down window is the 'time to readiness' flagged in the field.
  • The melt is decoupled from the moment of need by design: 'The mattresses can be heated in advance of use in the thermos (or oven).' A pre-melted pad held in its thermos/insulating sleeve stays at 37 C for about 6 hours (lab test: 7 h insulated, 2 h uninsulated in a 20 C room; field RCT: 5.5 h mean time of use, SD 2.9; warmer >35 C for >=6 h in 72% of 892 encounters). Once melted, a pad is ready for bedside use on the order of minutes: remove, dry, verify color indicator, slip into insulating pad.
  • No enforceable US utility patent exists on the warmer or its functional design: US17/000,314 ('Differential-melting point PCM as safety indicator for warming devices', filed 2020-08-22 by Regents of UC) is Abandoned and therefore public prior art.
  • Field/test results supporting manufacturability: 2018 pilot maintained goal temperature in >=85% of uses, corrected/prevented hypothermia in 95-100% of encounters, 2/12 warmers broke down after <10 uses; the 2021 stepped-wedge RCT (33 health facilities, 7,058 infants) reported infant mortality 0.9% vs 2.8% controls (OR 0.31, p=0.005), 99.5% of usable warmers still working after >1,000 uses, and a leakage fix via more robust plastic plus radiofrequency sealing.
