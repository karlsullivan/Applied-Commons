The need

About 2.2 billion people remain offline, most in low- and middle-income countries (ITU Facts and Figures 2025); a further 38% of the world's population lives within mobile broadband coverage but does not use it, and 93% of all unconnected people are in LMICs (GSMA SOMIC 2025). About 655 million people, disproportionately in Sub-Saharan Africa (560M+), have no electricity at all (IEA Tracking SDG7 2026), which caps any computing device. Affected need-holders: ~2.1 billion people without safely managed drinking water, 106M of whom drink surface water (WHO/UNICEF JMP 2025); displaced populations in clinics with intermittent connectivity (Hikma EHR deployed for 26,000 refugee patients; UNHCR 2019 displaced population over 80M per Frontiers 2022); smallholder and family farmers in rural areas who depend on agricultural advisory information (FAO Digital Services Portfolio); and roughly one third of the global population in countries without adequate multi-hazard early warning systems, concentrated in least developed countries and small island states (WMO Early Warnings for All). DHIS2-style open health platforms already serve ministries of health in 80+ LMICs, covering ~2.4 billion people (ITU SDG Digital; University of Oslo listing).

Severe, persistent, and worsening toward 2030. NCDs account for 74% of global deaths with 86% of premature NCD deaths in resource-constrained regions (WHO 2024 compendium); household air pollution from polluting fuels (used by ~2B people) caused 2.9M deaths in 2021, including 309,000 under-5s, and 1.8B people will still lack clean cooking by 2030 at current policy (IEA/WHO). The pace of electrification must triple to meet the 2030 target, and the rural electrification deficit in Sub-Saharan Africa grew from 376M (2010) to 447M (2024) (IEA 2026). Early warning systems save lives at nearly a tenfold return on investment, yet only half of countries report adequate multi-hazard systems (WMO) - so the absence of local compute and data infrastructure translates directly into ongoing preventable mortality, lost harvests, and broken continuity of care, not just inconvenience.

What people do now

Clinics and community health workers rely on paper registers and manual aggregation; DHIS2 digitizes national reporting but HISP/ITU note reaching the lowest levels of health systems is 'complicated and costly' and LMICs lack IT architecture planners to maintain such systems (ITU SDG Digital, 2023). For cooking and basic energy, ~2 billion people burn wood, charcoal, dung and kerosene in open fires or inefficient stoves, with women and girls bearing the fuel-gathering time burden (WHO household air pollution fact sheet). For farming information, FAO runs a cloud-based Digital Services Portfolio that pushes advisories to smartphone web apps - reachable only where data connectivity exists (FAO). For hazard information, communities without internet fall back on community radio, word of mouth, and national broadcasting, and one in three people lack access to adequate early warning at all (UN/WMO). The connectivity itself is unaffordable for most: the top barriers to adoption are affordability and digital literacy, and a US$30 handset would make phones affordable to 1.6 billion people - implying most of the unconnected cannot even buy the cheapest smartphone (GSMA SOMIC 2025); only 54% of the world owns a smartphone.

Where existing solutions fall short

Existing solutions cluster at the connected, urban, high-income end. Cloud-first platforms (FAO DSP, many mHealth and e-government apps) assume the reliable connectivity that the 2.2 billion offline do not have. Offline-first open software is rare: Hikma is a proven case but a narrow refugee-care EHR, and the field lacks an equivalent open, offline-first platform for agriculture, water, and early warning. Early warning is weakest at the last mile - only half of countries report adequate multi-hazard systems and one in three people lack access entirely (WMO/UN). Health digitization stops at the district level: DHIS2 covers 80+ countries but reaching the lowest levels of the health system is costly and LMICs lack local IT staff, so paper pipelines persist in the field (ITU/HISP). Energy is the binding constraint on all of it - 655M people have no power, and low-income countries generate just 33.6 W of renewable electricity per person vs 1,224 W in high-income countries (IEA 2026), so off-grid solar-powered compute is underdeveloped. Finally, affordability blocks the bottom of the market: a US$30 handset would unlock 1.6 billion people, meaning entry-level devices and data remain unaffordable for most of the unconnected (GSMA SOMIC 2025).

What a solution must do

  • Health: an offline-first, open-source patient-record system that keeps continuity of care with zero network connectivity and syncs opportunistically, runnable on a low-end smartphone from battery power for a full day of field work - demonstrated at 26,000-patient scale for refugee care by the open-source Hikma Health EHR (Frontiers 2022).
  • Health: a free, open-source, no-code-customizable national data platform hostable in-country and operated by the ministry of health, covering immunization, maternal/child, NCD and program monitoring for the ~2.4 billion people in DHIS2-using countries (ITU/HISP; DHIS2 in 80+ LMICs).
  • Food: offline-capable agricultural advisory delivery that reaches smallholders on feature phones and low-cost smartphones (SMS/voice/IVR or pre-loaded content), not only via cloud web apps, so crop, pest and market advice reaches farmers outside mobile broadband coverage (FAO DSP gap).
  • Safety: low-cost last-mile multi-hazard early warning that delivers alerts in local languages via SMS, voice and radio to internet-less communities, meeting the WMO/UNDRR four-pillar framework where WMO estimates ~10x return on investment (WMO EW4All).
  • Energy: field compute and storage that runs on small off-grid renewable power - e.g. a kit that powers a tablet or small server 8+ hours/day on a solar-plus-battery system, since 655M people have no grid electricity and 447M rural Sub-Saharan Africans lack it (IEA 2026).
  • Affordability: hardware and service cost within the reach of the unconnected majority - a US$30-class handset per user and data/service fees low enough that affordability is no longer the top adoption barrier (GSMA SOMIC 2025; ITU: 2.2B offline, progress slowing).
  • Openness: open-source code and open, disaggregated local datasets (health, water, food, hazard) stored and served locally, so governments and local developers can maintain, extend and repair them without vendor lock-in or license fees - the DHIS2/Hikma model - and reflecting the open-data approach of the WHO/UNICEF JMP for the 2.1 billion without safely managed water (washdata.org).
  • Usability: content in local languages with minimal digital-literacy requirements, since literacy and digital skills are a top barrier to adoption (GSMA SOMIC 2025).

Constraints

  • Power: 655M people have no electricity and the rural deficit in Sub-Saharan Africa is growing (447M in 2024), so devices must run on battery and small off-grid solar, not grid or mains power (IEA 2026).
  • Devices: only 54% of the global population owns a smartphone, so software must also run on feature phones and very low-cost hardware (GSMA SOMIC 2025).
  • Connectivity: 2.2B people are offline and connectivity progress is slowing; 38% of the population is covered but not using the network, so any solution cannot assume reliable data (ITU 2025; GSMA 2025).
  • Local capacity: LMICs lack IT architecture planners and sustained local IT infrastructure support, so systems must be maintainable locally with no-code tools and simple documentation (ITU/HISP DHIS2 interview).
  • Skills: digital literacy is a top adoption barrier; solutions need local-language, low-literacy interfaces (GSMA SOMIC 2025).
  • Data governance: health and personal data need local ownership; cloud-first designs (e.g. FAO DSP) route community data through external clouds, which conflicts with local data-sovereignty needs.
  • Scale economics: reaching the lowest levels of rural service delivery is costly, and financing for the poorest countries is declining (IEA 2026), favoring open, reusable, self-maintainable systems over bespoke commercial deployments.
